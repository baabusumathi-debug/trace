Section 1

What counts as cardiogenic shock, and where does it come from?

What this section settles
  1. Shock is a tissue diagnosis: perfusion too low for oxygen demand. Cardiogenic = a primary cardiac cause, proven by clinical AND biochemical hypoperfusion.
  2. SHOCK trial: SBP <90 for ≥30 min (or support), urine <30 mL/h or cool limbs, CI ≤2.2 with PCWP ≥15. ESC 2021 needs hypoperfusion, not hypotension.
  3. Two questions: 'Is it shock?' → hypoperfusion (lactate). 'Is it cardiogenic?' → high wedge with low index.
  4. SCAI A–E turns on hypoperfusion: B has low BP without it; C has it and needs more than volume.
  5. Muscle causes: alcohol (persists after thiamine), Takotsubo (no inotrope if LVOT obstruction), PPCM, endocrine; stunned: post-CABG low output, arrest, sepsis, contusion.
  6. Non-muscle causes: valve, rhythm, obstruction. AF in aortic stenosis = lost atrial kick. Adrenal crisis is distributive, contusion is contractility.
Carry this

Read the opening lesson

Two patients arrive in your ICU within the same hour, and their monitors read exactly the same. A blood pressure of eighty-two over fifty. A heart rate of one hundred and ten.

Patient A had an anterior infarct this morning. His hands are cold and wet, his urine has almost stopped, and he has crackles halfway up both lungs. Patient B has pneumonia. Her hands are warm, her pulse is bounding, and her chest is clear.

Same pressure. Opposite problems. And if you give them the same treatment, one of them gets worse.

So let's ask what a blood pressure actually is. The pressure in the arteries is the flow the heart pushes into them, multiplied by how hard the vessels squeeze back. Flow times resistance. And flow itself depends on two things: how much blood comes back to fill the heart, and how strongly the heart pumps it out.

That gives you three numbers. Filling, pump and squeeze. Preload, contractility and systemic vascular resistance.

Every shock is one of three numbers failing: the filling, the pump or the squeeze. Everything in this packet is a way of reading which one.

When one of them fails, the other two try to cover for it, and that pattern of covering is what names the shock. Patient A's pump has failed. Output falls, so his body clamps down on its vessels, and that's why his hands are cold. Blood can't get through the failing left ventricle, so it backs up into his lungs, and that's why he's wet. Patient B is the other way round. Her squeeze has failed. Her vessels have opened, her heart pumps harder to fill them, and that's why she's warm.

Now, how does NEET ask this? In four disguises, and they're the four parts of this packet.

First, definitions. What exactly counts as cardiogenic shock, and which numbers the trials used. Second, the bedside. Warm or cold, wet or dry, and why a failing pump makes itself worse. Third, the catheter. A table of four pressures and flows that names the shock. That one card is the most-asked cardiology card in your question banks, thirty questions, so we'll take it slowly. And fourth, the probe. You'll learn echo here, once and properly, with the model beside you, because every later packet will lean on it. Then we finish with the question you face at every shocked bedside: will fluid help?

One promise before we start. Shock Two will take the same three numbers and ask what pushes on each one: which drug, which receptor, which machine. So everything you learn to read here, you'll learn to treat there.

Shock is a tissue diagnosis

Carry this
Cardiogenic shock is a cardiac disorder with both clinical and biochemical evidence of tissue hypoperfusion.
Read the lesson behind this card

You've just been given the idea for this packet. Every shock is three numbers failing: filling, pump and squeeze. Before we read those numbers, we need to know what we're naming. So start with the word shock itself.

Shock is inadequate organ perfusion to meet the tissues' oxygen demand. Notice what that definition leaves out. It doesn't mention blood pressure. Shock is defined at the tissue. A normal pressure with a rising lactate is shock. A low pressure with warm hands, a clear head and a good urine output may not be.

Cardiogenic shock is that same state, critical end-organ hypoperfusion and hypoxia, caused by a primary cardiac disorder. The word primary matters. In sepsis the heart is depressed too, but there it's a casualty of another disease, not the cause. In our three numbers, cardiogenic shock is the one where the pump fails first, and the other two only react.

And the clinical definition asks for proof on two sides. Cardiogenic shock is a cardiac disorder with both clinical and biochemical evidence of tissue hypoperfusion. Why both? Because cold, clammy skin alone can be fear or a cold room, and a raised lactate alone can be adrenaline or a failing liver. Asking for both makes the diagnosis specific.

The bank tests this card from the other end. An asthmatic with wheeze, a heart rate of one hundred, a pressure of one hundred and thirty over eighty and a CVP of six. The tempting answer is some form of shock, because he looks sick. But nothing in that stem is under-perfused. It's acute asthma without shock.

So shock is a tissue diagnosis. Now, the trials had to turn that into numbers.

Read the lesson behind this card

A definition in words won't enrol patients in a trial. In nineteen ninety-nine the SHOCK trial, which tested early revascularisation in infarct shock, wrote the definition in numbers, and those are still the numbers examiners quote.

It has two halves. The clinical half first. A systolic pressure below ninety millimetres of mercury for at least thirty minutes. Why thirty minutes? To exclude the brief dip of a vagal reaction or a drug bolus. Or, as the alternative, support needed to keep the systolic at ninety or above. So a patient on norepinephrine with a pressure of one hundred still qualifies, because the drug is hiding the shock. Then add end-organ hypoperfusion: a urine output below thirty millilitres an hour, or cool extremities.

Then the haemodynamic half, and here our three numbers appear for the first time. The SHOCK trial needed a cardiac index of two point two litres per minute per square metre or less, together with a wedge pressure of fifteen millimetres of mercury or more. Read that as two statements. The low index says the pump is failing. The high wedge says the ventricle is full, so filling is not the problem. A low index alone proves nothing about the heart, because a bled patient has a low index too. That's the wrong option to watch for here: a low cardiac index, by itself, defines cardiogenic shock.

One thing about how it's asked. Many textbooks, and one grand test, split the index: below one point eight without support, below two point two on support. Accept either in an option. The numbers that never change are ninety for thirty minutes, and a wedge of fifteen.

That's the definition with a catheter in it. The guideline definition manages without one.

The SHOCK definition needs a catheter. The ESC heart failure guideline defines the same state from the bedside and the laboratory, and the best question on this section comes from it.

The card gives the wording of twenty sixteen. A systolic pressure below ninety with adequate volume, plus clinical or laboratory signs of hypoperfusion. Adequate volume is there to make you exclude an empty circulation first.

The lists matter more than the pressure. Five clinical signs: cold extremities, oliguria, mental confusion, dizziness and a narrow pulse pressure. Each one is the body moving flow away from skin, kidney and brain, and the narrow pulse pressure is simply a small stroke volume. Three laboratory signs: metabolic acidosis, a raised lactate and a raised creatinine.

Now the update. The ESC guideline of twenty twenty-one kept those lists but dropped the demand for hypotension. It defines cardiogenic shock as primary cardiac dysfunction causing life-threatening tissue hypoperfusion, and it says plainly that hypoperfusion is not always accompanied by a low pressure. Why? Because the squeeze number can hide the failure. Vasoconstriction, or a vasopressor, holds the pressure up while the tissues starve. That's normotensive cardiogenic shock. So if an option says the ESC still requires a systolic below ninety, recognise it as the old wording.

Here's the question that fewer than half answered. After an infarct: a cardiac index of one point nine, a wedge of nineteen, oliguria and a raised lactate. Which finding separates cardiogenic shock from heart failure alone? The favourite wrong option is one of the catheter numbers. But decompensated heart failure has exactly the same low index and the same high wedge. What turns heart failure into cardiogenic shock is hypoperfusion, and the raised lactate is the finding that shows it. The catheter says the pump is the cause. Only the tissues say it's shock.

Read the lesson behind this card

Turn the last question round. There, the heart failure was given and you had to prove shock. Here the shock is given, and you have to prove the heart did it.

In practice, cardiogenic shock is diagnosed on three clinical criteria. First, persistent hypotension, a systolic below ninety. Persistent means sustained, the thirty minutes of the trials, not a single reading. Second, no adequate response to volume resuscitation. That's the bedside test of our filling number: an empty circulation recovers with fluid, and a failing pump doesn't, and may get worse. Third, end-organ hypoperfusion, clinical and biochemical. Both, as before.

Now the grand test stem. An anterior infarct, a systolic of eighty-six for forty minutes on norepinephrine, a cardiac index of one point eight, a wedge of twenty, and a urine output of twenty millilitres an hour. Which finding confirms that this shock is cardiogenic? The tempting options are cold extremities, a narrow pulse pressure, or a lactate of two. But every low-output shock is cold with a narrow pulse pressure, a bled patient included. And lactate tells you there is shock, not where it came from. A wedge pressure of fifteen or more with a low cardiac index is what pins the shock on the left ventricle, because it proves the ventricle is full and still not ejecting.

So keep the two questions apart, because the examiner swaps them. Is this shock? Hypoperfusion answers, and lactate is its marker. Is this shock cardiogenic? A high filling pressure with a low output answers.

How far along is it? You met the SCAI stages in the cardiogenic shock walk, so this is a recall, pointed at MCQs.

Five stages, A to E, and the whole scale turns on one thing, hypoperfusion. Stage A is at risk: a large infarct or acute heart failure, but no signs yet. Stage B is beginning: relative hypotension or a tachycardia, without hypoperfusion, so the lactate is normal. Stage C is classic: hypoperfusion that needs an intervention beyond volume, meaning an inotrope, a vasopressor or mechanical support. Stage D is deteriorating: like C, but failing the first interventions. Stage E is extremis: on several supports at once, perhaps in arrest with CPR or ECMO running.

The line between stage B and stage C is hypoperfusion, not blood pressure. So the wrong option to expect is that hypotension defines stage C. A patient with a pressure of eighty-eight over sixty who is warm, with a lactate of one point four and a good urine output, is stage B. A normotensive patient with a lactate of four can be stage C. It's the same lesson as the last three cards, turned into a scale.

Three details from the current version, the update of twenty twenty-two, in case they appear. Stage C carries a lactate of two millimoles per litre or more. Stage E carries a lactate of eight or more, with a pH below seven point two. And the arrest modifier, the letter A, was narrowed. On the card it's any CPR, including defibrillation. In the update it's an arrest with coma afterwards, because a brief arrest with prompt waking doesn't carry the same risk.

What breaks the pump? The commonest answer is an infarct, and the coronary packet owns that. This card is the muscle that fails with clean coronaries, and it's asked often, because in each stem the history gives the cause away.

First, heart failure itself, in two forms. Chronic heart failure of known cause that decompensates, or acute heart failure as the first presentation.

Then four muscle diseases: chronic ischaemia, dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, and stress-induced cardiomyopathy, Takotsubo. Two of these carry the questions.

Alcohol first, because two thirds of the cohort missed it. A chronic drinker with confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia is given thiamine, and the hypotension persists. The wrong picks were neurogenic shock and sepsis. But Wernicke's has no cord lesion to cut the sympathetic outflow, and nothing in the stem points to infection. The answer is alcoholic dilated cardiomyopathy: heavy drinking, usually quoted as more than eighty grams a day for more than five years, giving a large, poorly contracting ventricle. The pump number has failed, so the patient is cold, and needs inotropes. Its mimic is wet beriberi, also in a drinker, also heart failure. Wet beriberi is warm, high-output failure with a low SVR that reverses within hours of thiamine. Alcoholic cardiomyopathy is cold, low-output failure that persists after thiamine.

Then Takotsubo. A woman, usually past the menopause, has chest pain after a bereavement. ST elevation or deep T inversion, a modest troponin rise. It looks like an anterior infarct. The discriminator is the angiogram: open coronaries, with an apex that balloons and a base that contracts hard, a pattern wider than any single coronary territory. It recovers over weeks. And here's the trap in its treatment. If that hyperkinetic base obstructs the outflow tract, an inotrope makes the obstruction worse. So Takotsubo with hypotension and outflow obstruction gets fluid, phenylephrine and a beta-blocker, not dobutamine.

Next, two causes that belong to pregnancy: peripartum cardiomyopathy and coronary artery dissection. Peripartum cardiomyopathy is systolic failure, with an ejection fraction below forty-five percent, late in pregnancy or in the months after delivery. One bank asked the favourable signs at diagnosis, and the answer was all of them: a left ventricle less than about five and a half to six centimetres in diastole, an ejection fraction above thirty to thirty-five percent, no troponin rise and no thrombus in the ventricle.

And two endocrine causes. Thyroid disease, too little or too much, and phaeochromocytoma, whose catecholamine surge can look exactly like Takotsubo.

The last card was muscle that failed from within. This one is muscle stunned from outside. Five causes, and in most of them the pump number recovers if you support it and don't make it worse.

Start with the one that's asked, post-cardiotomy shock. Its two mechanisms are a prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass and insufficient cardioprotection. Ischaemia during the cross-clamp, then reperfusion, stun the myocardium, and the output falls over the first hours, classically to a low point around four to six hours after bypass, recovering over the next day. So the stem reads: eight hours after an on-pump bypass operation, confused, cold, clammy and oliguric. That's low cardiac output syndrome.

What do you do? Think in our three numbers, in order. Optimise the filling and the afterload. Correct potassium, magnesium and calcium. Fix the rate and the rhythm, with pacing if needed. Then an inotrope, dobutamine or milrinone. Then a balloon pump or another device if that fails. And all the while exclude the two surgical causes, tamponade and graft ischaemia. The wrong option is high-dose vasoconstrictors alone. Raising the squeeze against a stunned ventricle lowers the output further, so after cardiac surgery you optimise loading, electrolytes and rhythm, then add an inotrope.

One mimic here. Tamponade after cardiac surgery is also cold and oliguric. What separates it is that the filling pressures rise and equalise while the chest drains fall silent. That patient needs re-opening, not an inotrope.

Now the other four, briefly. Dynamic outflow tract obstruction, where a small, hard-working ventricle pulls the mitral leaflet into its own outflow. You've just met it in Takotsubo. Post-cardiac-arrest stunning, which depresses the heart for one to three days and usually recovers. Myocardial depression in septic shock, which is reversible and secondary. That's why it sits on this card and yet doesn't count as primary cardiogenic shock, the word we stressed at the very start.

And myocardial contusion. One bank asks its screening rule. Every significant blunt chest injury gets an ECG and a troponin. If either is abnormal, monitor for twenty-four to forty-eight hours for arrhythmia, and add an echo if the patient is unstable. If both are normal, significant injury is excluded. So a stable patient with ectopic beats on the ECG after chest trauma is admitted for monitoring.

A heart can fail with healthy muscle. This last card of causes gives the three ways: a valve, a rhythm, or a block to flow. No bank asked it as a list. They asked its edges, so that's where we'll spend the time.

Valves first. On a native valve: stenosis, acute regurgitation, or obstruction of the valve. Acute regurgitation is the deadly one, because a ventricle of normal size suddenly has to take a large regurgitant volume, and its filling pressure soars. On a prosthetic valve: obstruction by thrombus or pannus, leaflet failure or restriction, mechanical failure, and dehiscence.

Then rhythm. An atrial arrhythmia with a rapid ventricular rate, ventricular tachycardia, or bradycardia. A fast rate cuts the filling time. A slow one cuts the number of beats.

Join those two branches, because a recent grand test did, and it was missed. A patient with aortic stenosis goes into atrial fibrillation and collapses. Why? The tempting answer was coronary thrombosis. The key was loss of the atrial kick. A ventricle thickened by aortic stenosis is stiff, so it fills poorly in early diastole and depends on atrial contraction for the last part of its filling. In aortic stenosis, new atrial fibrillation removes the atrial kick that the stiff ventricle depends on, so the filling number collapses even though the muscle is unchanged. In a normal heart the atrial kick gives about a fifth of the filling. In a stiff one it can be nearer two fifths.

Then the block to flow. The card lists cardiac tamponade, constriction and pulmonary embolism. The wider list of obstructive shock adds tension pneumothorax. And obstruction to forward flow, as one bank words it, includes critical aortic stenosis, dynamic outflow obstruction in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, mitral stenosis and a left atrial myxoma.

Two edges are tested. Which of these is not obstructive shock? Adrenal crisis. It's distributive, a failure of squeeze, with a low SVR. And which does not obstruct forward flow? Myocardial contusion, because a bruised muscle is a pump problem, not a block.

The card ends with two others: toxidromes, such as beta-blocker and calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, and the myocardial depression of hypothermia.

The spiral: the rescue is the problem

Carry this
A persistently high SVR harms the injured heart through a higher afterload: wall stress and oxygen demand go up, and stroke volume goes down.
Read the lesson behind this card

Whatever broke the pump, the output has now fallen. This card explains why cardiogenic shock then gets worse by itself. In our three numbers: the pump fails first, and the body answers by pushing on the other two, filling and squeeze. Each push adds load to a heart that's already injured.

Follow the chain. Myocardial injury reduces the cardiac output. Two sensors notice. The carotid baroreceptors are stretched less, so they stop restraining the sympathetic outflow. And the kidney is perfused less, so it releases renin. Two systems switch on: the sympathetic nervous system, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

Take the sympathetic system first. It raises the heart rate and the inotropy. That sounds helpful, but both raise the oxygen demand of a heart that's short of supply, and catecholamines are directly toxic to myocytes. It's the limb that beta-blockers inhibit.

Then renin, which has two limbs. Angiotensin two constricts the vessels, and that raises the afterload. That's the squeeze. Aldosterone holds on to salt and water, and that raises the preload. That's the filling. These are the limbs blocked by ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and aldosterone antagonists, and the card adds ADH antagonists for the water.

Both systems arrive at the same place: negative remodelling and a worse left ventricle. Only one thing pushes back. BNP and ANP, released by the stretched chambers, oppose the vasoconstriction and the fluid retention. Keep that in mind; it's why BNP is a test for a stretched heart.

Now the question. A large anterior infarct in shock, and the SVR stays high even after reperfusion. What worsens the myocardial injury? The distractors are reduced oxygen extraction and capillary recruitment, which sound physiological and mean nothing here. A persistently high SVR harms the injured heart through a higher afterload: wall stress and oxygen demand go up, and stroke volume goes down.

Keep the two harms distinct, because options trade on them. Afterload raises wall stress and cuts the stroke volume. Preload raises the filling pressure and floods the lungs.

One honest exception. In about a fifth of infarct shock, a systemic inflammatory response dilates the vessels instead, so the SVR isn't always high. That patient is warm when you expected cold.

In the nineteen seventies James Forrester and his colleagues put the new pulmonary artery catheter into patients after myocardial infarction and sorted them by two numbers: a cardiac index of two point two litres per minute per square metre, and a wedge pressure of eighteen millimetres of mercury. Four groups fell out. Mortality was a few percent with both numbers normal, and about half with both abnormal.

Today the same grid is read in bedside words. Cold or warm is perfusion. Wet or dry is congestion. And the shock version on your card adds the third number, the SVR.

Cold and wet is classic cardiogenic shock. The index is low, the SVR is high as the body clamps down, and the wedge is high. About two thirds of infarct shock sits here.

Cold and dry is euvolaemic cardiogenic shock. A low index and a high SVR, but a normal wedge. This is the one cardiogenic patient who may take a small, careful fluid bolus.

Warm and wet is vasodilatory cardiogenic shock, or mixed shock. The index is still low, but the SVR is low or normal, and the wedge is high. It's the inflamed infarct from the last card, or an infarct with sepsis.

Warm and dry has a high index, a low SVR and a low wedge. That's vasodilatory shock, and it isn't cardiogenic at all.

Three quadrants have a low cardiac index, and that is what makes them cardiogenic. Warm and dry is the only one with a high index, and it is not cardiogenic shock.

FATE was the method. This card is the work-up of cardiogenic shock itself, five tests, with echo first because it answers the most.

On echo you seek four things: left and right ventricular dysfunction, valvular abnormalities, chamber dimensions, and the response to therapy. That last one is the point people forget. The model shows the commonest finding, and one bank asks it plainly. A heart failure patient, hypotensive, with a poor ejection fraction on echo, has cardiogenic shock from acute left-sided failure. Barely half got it. Don't hunt for a cleverer answer.

A grand test asked: all are true about Kerley lines, except? Only one in five found it. The false statement was that they represent swollen lymphatics. Pictures first, numbers last. Doppler measures velocity, and nothing else. Every pressure you'll ever quote from an echo comes out of one equation.

When blood is forced through a narrow opening it speeds up, and pressure is traded for velocity. The simplified Bernoulli equation says the pressure gradient, in millimetres of mercury, is four times the velocity squared, with the velocity in metres per second. A grand test asked which equation gives the pressure gradient between two chambers, and fewer than half got it. The wrong pick was Laplace. Laplace relates wall tension to pressure and radius. Poiseuille relates flow to the radius and length of a tube. Bernoulli turns a velocity into a pressure gradient: four times the velocity squared.

Now the card's normal values, in three groups.

Continuous-wave Doppler, which measures high velocities. Across the aortic valve, below two metres per second. Across the pulmonary valve, below one. And a tricuspid regurgitant jet below two point five. Put that jet into Bernoulli and add the right atrial pressure from the IVC, and you have the right ventricular systolic pressure, the calculation you did with the IVC. A tricuspid velocity above two point eight raises the probability of pulmonary hypertension.

Pulsed-wave Doppler, at the tips of the mitral leaflets. There are two waves. E is early, passive filling. A is atrial contraction, the atrial kick whose loss sank the patient with aortic stenosis. Normally E is below one point two metres per second, the ratio of E to A is above one, though that falls with age, and the deceleration time of the E wave is one hundred and forty to two hundred and forty milliseconds. When relaxation is impaired, E falls and A rises. When the left atrial pressure is high, E is tall and stops abruptly.

And the third group, which the card labels TVI. Here that means tissue velocity imaging, not the velocity time integral. It measures how fast the mitral ring itself moves in early diastole, written e prime. Divide E by e prime and you track the left atrial pressure. In our three numbers, that's the filling of the left heart, the job the wedge did on the catheter. The card's normal is below eight to ten. The figure in current guidelines is that an average above fourteen means a raised filling pressure.

The IVC gave a hint and no more. To see why, sort every test of fluid responsiveness into three families. You met them in haemodynamics, so this is a recall with the numbers.

The question behind all of them is the slope. Is this heart on the steep part of its Frank-Starling curve, where more filling gives more output, or on the flat part, where it gives only congestion?

The first family is static. One pressure or one size, taken at one moment: the CVP, the wedge pressure, the diameter of the IVC or the superior vena cava, the corrected flow time, and the end-diastolic volumes. Their area under the ROC curve is about zero point five to zero point six, and zero point five is a coin toss. Why so poor? Because a single point tells you where the patient is, not the slope he's on. The same filling pressure sits on the steep part in one heart and the flat part in another.

The second family is dynamic. Each ventilator breath is a small, repeated change in filling, and you watch how far the stroke volume swings. Pulse pressure variation, stroke volume variation, the pleth variability index, aortic blood flow variation, and the respiratory variation of the IVC. Their area is about zero point seven to zero point eight.

The third family is the challenge. You actually move the patient along his curve and measure what happens. A real one, a rapid fluid challenge of one hundred to two hundred and fifty millilitres. Or a virtual one, the passive leg raise. Their area is about zero point nine.

Static measures are near a coin toss, dynamic indices are good, and a challenge, real or virtual, is best. The more directly a test probes the slope, the better it performs.

So the wrong option to expect is a filling pressure offered as the best predictor. A CVP, or a wedge, however carefully measured, is a static number. And notice where the IVC belongs. Its diameter is static. Its change with breathing is dynamic.

The best family includes a test that needs no fluid at all.

The best family was the challenge, and the leg raise is the challenge you can take back. You learned it in haemodynamics. It's here again because a recent grand test showed it as an image, and it was missed.

The method. Start with the patient semi-recumbent, trunk at forty-five degrees, not flat. Then use the bed to tip him, so the trunk goes flat and the legs come up. Why start semi-recumbent? Because lowering the trunk also empties the large splanchnic reservoir, not just the legs. Together that's about three hundred millilitres of his own blood. And you tilt with the bed, without touching him, because pain or arousal raises the output by itself.

The effect arrives within sixty to ninety seconds, and it reverses fully when you return him to the start.

Now the part the question turns on. What do you measure? Not the blood pressure. A real rise in stroke volume may hardly move the pressure, so you'd miss true responders. You measure cardiac output or stroke volume in real time: pulse contour analysis, the velocity time integral on echo, or oesophageal Doppler. A passive leg raise is positive when cardiac output or stroke volume rises by ten percent or more. With end-tidal carbon dioxide as the surrogate, the threshold is a rise of five percent or more.

So to the image. The stroke volume was thirteen percent higher after the leg raise. The wrong pick was an artefact of tachycardia. But thirteen is above ten, and that's the whole question. The patient is fluid responsive.

And why does this test matter more than the others? It doesn't use the ventilator, and it doesn't use the rhythm. The leg raise stays valid with spontaneous breathing, with arrhythmias and at low tidal volume, exactly where pulse pressure variation fails.

One caution. With the velocity time integral, a responder shows a rise of ten to fifteen percent. A change of a few percent is within the error of the measurement.

You started with Patient A and Patient B. The same pressure, opposite problems. And you now have three numbers that tell them apart. Filling, pump and squeeze.
