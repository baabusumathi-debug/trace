Acute pancreatitis

Before you startThe pancreas makes its digestive enzymes as inactive precursors (zymogens) and keeps a trypsin inhibitor inside the acinar cell, because trypsin is the enzyme that switches all the others on; acute pancreatitis is what happens when that protection fails. The disease runs in two phases: in the first week the harm comes from a sterile systemic inflammatory response and organ failure, and after that from local complications such as necrosis and its infection. Severity is therefore defined by organ failure and its duration, measured with the modified Marshall score (respiratory, cardiovascular and renal systems, a score of 2 or more is failure). Figures here follow the revised Atlanta classification (2012) and the ACG 2024 guideline, which the exam keys use.

The gut in critical illness

Before you startThe gut is rarely the reason a patient is admitted to the ICU, but it is affected in most of them, and unlike the kidney or lung it has no single number that tells you it is failing. Its blood flow is the first to be cut when cardiac output falls, because splanchnic vasoconstriction protects the brain and heart, and it is often the last to recover. Feeding, motility and the mucosal barrier are linked: a gut that is not fed loses its villi, and a gut that cannot move cannot be fed. Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) is measured through the bladder; 12 mmHg or more is intra-abdominal hypertension (GI05).

Mesenteric ischaemia

Before you startMesenteric ischaemia is uncommon (about 0.1–0.2% of emergency admissions) but kills most of those in whom it is missed, because the window between reversible mucosal ischaemia and transmural infarction is measured in hours. The early patient has severe pain and a soft abdomen; by the time there is guarding, lactate and gas in the bowel wall, the bowel is dead. The ICU sees it in two ways: as the cause of an acute abdomen, and as a complication of shock and vasopressors (non-occlusive disease) in a sedated patient who cannot describe pain. This deck runs from anatomy to the operating table.

Abdominal pressure

Before you startThe abdomen is a closed box whose walls stretch only so far: once its compliance is used up, a small rise in volume produces a large rise in pressure. That pressure is passed on to everything inside (kidneys, gut, liver, the inferior vena cava) and, through the diaphragm, to the chest. Perfusion of an abdominal organ depends on the gap between arterial pressure and the pressure around it, just as cerebral perfusion depends on MAP minus ICP. All figures in this deck are in mmHg; 1 mmHg is about 1.36 cmH2O.

    Acute pancreatitis Cause and diagnosis
    Every cause ends in trypsin switched on inside the acinar cell; two of three criteria make the diagnosis, and lipase is the enzyme to trust.
    The concept, in order
    1. Gallstones (40–70%) then alcohol (25–35%) cause most attacks. With neither, check triglycerides (causal above 1,000 mg/dL), calcium, drugs and a recent ERCP.
    2. Day-1 liver tests hint at the cause: ALT above 150 points to stones, AST above ALT to alcohol. So the first scan is an ultrasound for gallstones.
    3. Every route (duct obstruction, acinar injury, faulty enzyme transport) ends in trypsin activated inside the acinar cell, and trypsin switches the other enzymes on.
    4. Lipase causes fat necrosis, which saponifies calcium, so calcium falls; elastase digests vessel walls and causes bleeding.
    5. Diagnosis is two of three: typical pain, lipase or amylase above 3× normal, typical CT or MRI. With pain and enzymes, no imaging is needed.
    6. Lipase is the better test: pancreas-specific, up in 4–8 h and raised for 8–14 days. Amylase comes from many tissues and normalises in 3–4 days, so a late presenter can have a normal amylase.
    7. The enzyme level does not grade severity. 80–85% of attacks are mild (mortality under 1–3%); the 20% that are moderate or severe carry 13–35%.
    Key vs guideline
    • A raised amylase is not specific: a perforated ulcer or mesenteric ischaemia can raise it. When the picture does not fit, CT decides.

    PANC-01 Post-ERCP pancreatitis vs post-ERCP cholangitis or perforation
    Pancreatitis: epigastric pain within hours with enzymes above three times normal. Cholangitis: fever, jaundice, raised ALP and bilirubin. Perforation: free gas, often retroperitoneal, on imaging.
    PANC-01 Gallstone vs alcohol pancreatitis on day 1
    Gallstone: ALT above 150 with raised ALP and bilirubin, older woman, stones on ultrasound. Alcohol: AST above ALT, heavy drinking, often previous attacks.
    PANC-02 Low calcium from saponification vs from low albumin
    Saponification lowers ionised calcium; low albumin lowers total calcium only. Both occur, and neither reflects volume.
    PANC-03 Pancreatitis vs perforated ulcer with a raised amylase
    A perforation can raise amylase, usually below three times normal, and the X-ray shows free gas under the diaphragm. Sudden onset with board-like rigidity favours perforation.
    PANC-03 Pancreatitis vs aortic aneurysm or mesenteric ischaemia
    Pain eased by leaning forward with lipase above three times normal is pancreatitis (CGT4); an aneurysm or ischaemia does not raise lipase to that level and shows on CT angiography.
    PANC-04 Amylase raised from salivary gland vs pancreas
    Salivary (mumps, parotitis) raises amylase with a normal lipase; the pancreas raises both.
    PANC-03 “Do a contrast CT to confirm the diagnosis” 41% right
    With pain and lipase five times normal, two criteria are met; no imaging is needed initially (41% right). A CT on day 1 adds radiation and contrast, and underestimates necrosis, which takes about 72 hours to show (PANC-12). Ultrasound is still done, but to find gallstones, not to diagnose pancreatitis; in this stem there is ‘no biliary disease’, so the key is no imaging.
    PANC-01 Hypercalcaemia or pancreatic cancer as the ‘except’ answer 66% right
    Both are associated (calcium activates trypsinogen; a tumour blocks the duct). Hyperthyroidism is the one with no link (GT7, 66% right).
    PANC-01 “Post-ERCP pancreatitis is caused by contrast toxicity” 68% right
    The AIM1 key (68% right) is contrast toxicity, but the main mechanism is mechanical: papillary trauma and swelling from cannulation and hydrostatic pressure from injecting the pancreatic duct, with some chemical injury from contrast. Contrast toxicity is the best of the options offered, so accept it in that question.
    PANC-01 “Milky serum and high triglycerides in a pancreatitis means alcohol caused it”
    Alcohol can raise triglycerides modestly, but lactescent serum with very high triglycerides points to hyperlipoproteinaemia as the cause (CGT6).
    PANC-02 “Amylase activation is the initiating event”
    Trypsin is the trigger because it activates the others; amylase is a marker, not a cause of injury.
    More traps (2)
    PANC-04 “A higher enzyme level means more severe pancreatitis”
    Enzyme level has no prognostic value (also the GT8 explanation); it is a diagnostic test only.
    PANC-04 “Normal amylase on day 4 excludes pancreatitis”

Alcohol injures the acinar cell directly and makes secretions viscous so that protein plugs form in small ducts; it usually takes years of heavy drinking, and an attack in a heavy drinker is often on a background of chronic pancreatitis.     Why, part by part
    • a Rises: amylase 2–12 hours; lipase 4–8 hours. Both are up by the time most patients arrive, so timing of the rise rarely decides anything.
    • b Peaks: amylase 12–72 hours; lipase 24 hours.
    • c Normalises: amylase 3–4 days; lipase 8–14 days. Amylase is a small molecule cleared by the kidney with a half-life of about 10–12 hours, so it can be back to normal by the time a late presenter arrives; lipase is reabsorbed by the renal tubule and persists. This is why lipase is the test for someone who has had pain for several days.
    • d Specificity: amylase low (salivary gland, bowel, ovary, fat, muscle); lipase high. Amylase is made in many tissues; its specificity is only about 20–60%, while lipase reaches 80–100% at the diagnostic threshold. ACG 2024 prefers lipase and says amylase alone is unreliable; ordering both adds nothing over lipase.
    • e False positives: amylase in renal disease, appendicitis, cholecystitis; lipase fewer, not none — renal failure (both enzymes), bowel obstruction or ischaemia, perforated ulcer, cholecystitis, DKA. Bowel ischaemia and perforation leak enzymes from the gut lumen into the peritoneum; renal failure slows clearance. These rises are usually below three times normal, which is why the diagnostic threshold is set at three. In macroamylasaemia, amylase bound to immunoglobulin cannot be filtered: serum amylase is high and urine amylase low, with no pancreatitis.
    • f Urine amylase raised for up to 2 weeks; urine trypsinogen-2 dipstick sensitivity 80%, specificity 92%; enzyme level does not grade severity. Trypsinogen-2 is a small protein that is filtered and poorly reabsorbed, so it appears in urine within hours; the 80% and 92% are the pooled figures of an 18-study meta-analysis (2013), which makes the dipstick a quick bedside aid but not good enough to replace serum lipase. The size of the rise reflects how much enzyme leaked, not how much inflammation or necrosis there is, so a lipase of 10 times normal is not worse than 4 times. Severity comes from organ failure (PANC-05) and the scores (PANC-08 to PANC-11).

    Acute pancreatitis Grading and predicting severity
    Severity is defined by how long organs fail; the scores only predict it.
    The concept, in order
    1. Revised Atlanta (2012) grades by organ failure. Mild: none, and no complications. Moderately severe: organ failure that resolves within 48 h, and/or local or systemic complications. Severe: organ failure beyond 48 h.
    2. Organ failure means a modified Marshall score of 2 or more in the respiratory, cardiovascular or renal system: three systems only.
    3. The determinant-based system adds infection and has four grades; critical = persistent organ failure and infected necrosis. Infected necrosis alone is already severe there, but only moderately severe in Atlanta.
    4. Scores predict; they do not define. Ranson has 11 items, 5 at admission and 6 at 48 h, so it is complete only at 48 h. 3 or more predicts severe disease, 6 or more about 40% mortality, and it contains no amylase or lipase.
    5. BISAP is the bedside score in the first 24 h: BUN above 25, impaired mental state, SIRS, age above 60, pleural effusion. 3 or more marks a sharp rise in deaths.
    6. Single markers: APACHE II of 8 or more; CRP above 150 mg/L at 48 h predicts necrosis; haematocrit above 44% or a rising BUN on day 1 shows third-space loss.
    7. The CT severity index adds the Balthazar grade (0–4) to necrosis (0, 2, 4 or 6), for a maximum of 10. Necrosis weighs more, and 7–10 carries about 17% mortality.
    8. Cullen and Grey Turner signs (retroperitoneal blood) mean haemorrhagic necrotising disease but appear in under 5%: useful when present, useless when absent.
    Key vs guideline
    • BISAP cut-off: the notes give 2; most sources and the exam use 3 or more.
    • Age: above 55 in Ranson, above 60 in BISAP. A favourite swap.

    More pairs from the cards (10)
    PANC-05 Modified Marshall vs SOFA vs BISAP or Ranson
    Modified Marshall defines organ failure in revised Atlanta; SOFA does it in the determinant-based system; BISAP and Ranson predict severity and cannot define it.
    PANC-05 Transient vs persistent organ failure
    The same organ, failing for under 48 hours or for 48 hours or more. The grade cannot be final until 48 hours have passed.
    PANC-06 Determinant-based ‘severe’ vs Atlanta ‘severe’
    Determinant-based: persistent organ failure or infected necrosis. Atlanta: persistent organ failure only.
    PANC-08 Ranson vs BISAP
    Ranson: 11 items over 48 hours, lab-heavy. BISAP: 5 items within 24 hours, at the bedside (PANC-09). Ranson’s age cut-off is 55, BISAP’s 60.
    PANC-09 BISAP vs Ranson items
    BISAP: BUN, mental status, SIRS, age 60, pleural effusion. Ranson: age 55, white cells, glucose, AST, LDH, then haematocrit, BUN, calcium, PaO₂, base deficit, fluid. Haematocrit, calcium and LDH are Ranson, not BISAP.
    PANC-10 Necrotising vs interstitial oedematous pancreatitis on CT
    Necrotising: areas of the gland that do not enhance. Interstitial: the whole gland enhances, with surrounding stranding or fluid.
    PANC-10 Balthazar CTSI vs modified CTSI
    The modified index (Mortele) scores inflammation 0, 2 or 4, necrosis 0, 2 (up to 30%) or 4 (over 30%) and adds 2 for extrapancreatic complications; the card and the questions use Balthazar.
    PANC-11 Prediction vs classification
    APACHE II, CRP, BISAP and Ranson predict severity early; revised Atlanta classifies it, and only after 48 hours of organ failure.
    Top-up Grey Turner vs Cullen
    Grey Turner is flank bruising (retroperitoneal route); Cullen is periumbilical bruising (along the falciform ligament). Both mean retroperitoneal or intra-abdominal blood.
    Top-up Cullen in pancreatitis vs ruptured ectopic
    Pancreatitis: epigastric pain to the back, lipase above three times normal. Ectopic: a woman of reproductive age, positive pregnancy test, pelvic fluid on ultrasound.
    PANC-10 “Day 4, hypotension, rising white cells, CT: interstitial oedematous or haemorrhagic pancreatitis” 39% right
    Non-enhancing gland on a day-4 contrast CT in a deteriorating patient is necrosis (only 39% right). ‘Haemorrhagic pancreatitis’ is not an Atlanta category, and a pseudocyst cannot form before 4 weeks.
    PANC-11 “APACHE II 18 means moderate systemic illness” 46% right

    Acute pancreatitis Infection, lung and the tight abdomen
    Early fever is inflammation, not infection; infected necrosis comes late and gets a carbapenem. The lung fails first, and a tense abdomen changes how you ventilate.
    The concept, in order
    1. Deaths come in two waves: weeks 1–2 from SIRS and organ failure, after 2 weeks from infected necrosis.
    2. No prophylactic antibiotics, even in severe necrotising disease: they prevent neither infected necrosis nor death.
    3. Suspect infected necrosis with late deterioration (weeks 2–4) or gas in a collection on CT. Procalcitonin helps hold antibiotics early; routine needle aspiration is no longer advised.
    4. Then a carbapenem: it penetrates necrosis and covers ESBL Gram-negatives. Antifungals are not routine.
    5. The lung fails most often: SIRS, fluid and phospholipase A₂ digesting surfactant (DPPC) cause ARDS, often with a left pleural effusion. Try non-invasive support, then lung-protective ventilation.
    6. A tense abdomen passes 20–60% of its pressure to the chest, so the chest wall, not the lung, is stiff. Keep 6–8 mL/kg and driving pressure under 15, set PEEP by compliance (often higher), and allow a plateau target of target − 7 + 0.7 × IAP. An oesophageal balloon settles it.
    7. Pancreatic encephalopathy (confusion and myoclonus in the first 2 weeks) is a diagnosis of exclusion: rule out Wernicke’s (give thiamine), glucose, sodium and alcohol withdrawal.

    The gut in critical illness The gut barrier
    In the ICU the gut matters as a barrier: low flow kills the villus tip first, the barrier leaks, and toxic lymph reaches the lung.
    The concept, in order
    1. The gut absorbs, but in the ICU its barrier (mucosa, mucin) and immune roles (gut lymphoid tissue, secretory IgA) matter more.
    2. Each segment has its loss: duodenum iron and calcium; jejunum most nutrients; terminal ileum B12 and bile salts (resection → B12 deficiency and bile-salt diarrhoea); colon water, sodium, short-chain fatty acids.
    3. Splanchnic flow is cut first when output falls, and the villus tip dies first: the central arteriole fountains out at the tip, and counter-current exchange shunts oxygen to the venule before it arrives.
    4. So mucosal ischaemia comes before transmural ischaemia, and lactate rises late.
    5. The sequence: hypoperfusion → resuscitation → ischaemia–reperfusion and oedema → barrier loss. Bacteria and endotoxin then translocate into portal blood and lymph.
    6. Toxic lymph travels up the thoracic duct and reaches the lung first: the gut is the motor of multiple organ failure.
    7. The defence is early enteral feeding: it keeps the villi intact and cuts translocation.
    8. The diaphragm clears peritoneal fluid and bacteria through stomata into lymph on expiration, so peritoneal infection reaches the blood fast. IAH and ventilation splint it and slow clearance.

    The gut as the “motor” of multiple organ failure:
    a Loss of barrier function.
    b Changes in intestinal microbiota.
    c Translocation of bacteria and endotoxin to portal blood and lymphatics.
    d Formation of toxic lymph (reaches the lung first, via the thoracic duct).
    e Dysregulated apoptosis and proliferation.
    f Sequence: hypoperfusion, then resuscitation (fluids, vasopressors), then ischaemia–reperfusion and oedema, then barrier loss.

    Why, part by part
    • a Loss of barrier function. Hypoperfusion, reperfusion and oedema open the tight junctions and slough the villus tips (GUT-02). This is the step every exam answer hangs on: an infection from gut organisms in a critically ill patient is attributed to disruption of the mucosal barrier.
    • b Changes in intestinal microbiota. Antibiotics, acid suppression, opioids and absent feeding replace normal commensals with pathogenic, often resistant organisms (Enterococcus, Gram-negative bacilli, Candida). Overgrowth on its own does not cause sepsis; it supplies the organisms, and the broken barrier lets them through.
    • c Translocation of bacteria and endotoxin to portal blood and lymphatics. What crosses the mucosa reaches the liver through the portal vein, where Kupffer cells release cytokines, or the mesenteric lymph nodes. In injured patients, portal venous blood rarely grows bacteria, which is why the emphasis moved from whole bacteria in the portal blood to what travels in the lymph (the next part).
    • d Formation of toxic lymph (reaches the lung first, via the thoracic duct). Mesenteric lymph from the injured gut carries inflammatory lipids and proteins that bypass the liver and enter the systemic venous blood through the thoracic duct at the left venous angle (where the left subclavian and internal jugular veins join), so the pulmonary capillaries are the first bed they meet. This is the gut–lymph explanation for why ARDS so often follows shock and major abdominal surgery.
    • e Dysregulated apoptosis and proliferation. In sepsis the epithelium dies faster and renews more slowly, so the barrier cannot repair itself while the insult continues.
    • f Sequence: hypoperfusion, then resuscitation (fluids, vasopressors), then ischaemia–reperfusion and oedema, then barrier loss. Our treatment is part of the injury: reperfusion releases oxygen radicals, large volumes cause bowel oedema and raise IAP, and vasopressors keep splanchnic flow low. Enteral feeding works against the sequence: luminal nutrients feed enterocytes, keep villus height and stimulate IgA, which is the reason to feed early (the watch line).

Two DocT GT questions link here, one for each.
    Board-like rigid abdomen: perforated peptic ulcer vs bile peritonitis — clue, first test, treatment?
    • Perforated ulcer: sudden severe epigastric pain, shoulder-tip pain (diaphragm, C3–5), ulcer/NSAID history, shock
    • Erect CXR after 10 min upright shows free gas in ~75–85% (CT if negative) → resuscitate, NG, IV PPI, antibiotics, urgent omental-patch repair
    • Bile peritonitis: days after cholecystectomy, liver biopsy or trauma; generalised pain, ascites, ileus, rising bilirubin
    • Confirm by US/CT collection, HIDA leak or bile on aspiration → percutaneous drainage + ERCP stent/sphincterotomy; surgery if generalised peritonitis
    Why, part by part
    • a Perforated ulcer: sudden severe epigastric pain, shoulder-tip pain (diaphragm, C3–5), ulcer or NSAID history, shock. Acid and gastric contents flood the peritoneum at once, so the onset is abrupt and the abdomen becomes board-like; fluid under the diaphragm irritates phrenic afferents and the pain is referred to the shoulder.
    • b Erect chest X-ray after 10 minutes upright shows free gas in about 75–85% (CT if negative); then resuscitate, NG tube, IV PPI, antibiotics, urgent omental-patch repair. Ten minutes lets the gas rise under the diaphragm. A negative film does not exclude perforation: in one series the upright PA chest film found 80% of pneumoperitoneum and an upright lateral view 98%, and CT is the reference test. Repair is usually a Graham omental patch.
    • c Bile peritonitis: days after cholecystectomy, liver biopsy or trauma; generalised pain, ascites, ileus, rising bilirubin. Bile is less acutely irritant than acid, so the picture builds over days, and the peritoneum reabsorbs bilirubin, so the serum level rises.
    • d Confirm by ultrasound or CT collection, HIDA leak or bile on aspiration; then percutaneous drainage and ERCP stent or sphincterotomy; surgery if generalised peritonitis. Lowering the pressure in the bile duct with a stent lets the leak close; the collection is drained.

    • “A negative erect chest film excludes perforation” → It misses about one in five; do CT.

    Top-up · Clostridial gas gangrene of the abdomen DocT 1 questions
    Before the card: Step 3 includes the abdomen that is septic and full of gas. Gas in the bowel wall, mesentery and abdominal wall with crepitus and shock in a diabetic points to a gas-forming organism that destroys tissue. The one DocT QBank question asks which organism (77% right).
    Diabetic with peritonitis, shock, abdominal-wall crepitus and gas in bowel wall, mesentery and soft tissue: organism?
    • Clostridium perfringens: anaerobic, spore-forming Gram-positive rod
    • Alpha-toxin (lecithinase) → myonecrosis, haemolysis and shock
    • Immediate radical debridement + penicillin with clindamycin (stops toxin synthesis) ± hyperbaric O2
    • Gas without myonecrosis in diabetics (emphysematous pyelonephritis/cholecystitis) is usually E. coli/Klebsiella
    Why, part by part
    • a Clostridium perfringens: anaerobic, spore-forming Gram-positive rod. It grows in dead, poorly perfused tissue and ferments it, producing gas that tracks along tissue planes and gives crepitus.
    • b Alpha-toxin (lecithinase) causes myonecrosis, haemolysis and shock. Alpha-toxin is a phospholipase C that breaks down cell membranes, so muscle dies and red cells lyse; it also depresses the myocardium and causes profound shock.
    • c Immediate radical debridement plus penicillin with clindamycin (stops toxin synthesis), with or without hyperbaric oxygen. Antibiotics cannot reach dead tissue, so the dead tissue must go. Clindamycin inhibits protein synthesis and so stops toxin production even when bacteria are not dividing. Hyperbaric oxygen inhibits alpha-toxin production by this anaerobe, but must not delay surgery.
    • d Gas without myonecrosis in diabetics (emphysematous pyelonephritis or cholecystitis) is usually E. coli or Klebsiella. Gram-negative organisms ferment the high glucose in diabetic tissue and make gas, but they do not destroy muscle the way clostridia do.
    Traps on this card (1)
    • “E. coli or Klebsiella” for gas-forming peritonitis with shock and crepitus → They make gas in the kidney or gallbladder without myonecrosis; tissue destruction with shock is C. perfringens.
    Gas, crepitus, myonecrosis and shock mean C. perfringens: alpha-toxin kills tissue faster than antibiotics can reach it, so debride at once and give penicillin with clindamycin.

    Top-up · Perforation site and contamination Marrow 1 questions
    Before the card: Step 3 includes what a perforation spills. The bacterial load rises by many orders of magnitude from stomach to colon, so the site of perforation predicts how dirty the peritonitis is. The one Marrow question asks which site gives the greatest infection: the distal colon.
    Which perforation gives the worst faecal peritonitis, and why?
    • Bacterial load rises down the gut: stomach <10^3/mL (acid), jejunum ~10^4, ileum ~10^7–8, colon ~10^11–12
    • Colon flora is anaerobe-dominant (Bacteroides ~1000:1 over aerobes)
    • So distal colonic perforation = heaviest polymicrobial contamination → source control + cover for Gram-negatives and anaerobes
    • Gastroduodenal perforation is initially chemical peritonitis with a low bacterial load
    Why, part by part
    • a Bacterial load rises down the gut: stomach under 10³/mL (acid), jejunum about 10⁴, ileum about 10⁷–10⁸, colon about 10¹¹–10¹². Acid kills most swallowed organisms; transit keeps the jejunum relatively clean; stasis and the ileocaecal valve allow the colon to hold the densest bacterial population in the body.
    • b Colon flora is anaerobe-dominant (Bacteroides about 1000:1 over aerobes). That is why colonic peritonitis needs anaerobic cover.
    • c So distal colonic perforation gives the heaviest polymicrobial contamination: source control plus cover for Gram-negatives and anaerobes. Faecal peritonitis has the highest rate of abscess and septic shock.
    • d Gastroduodenal perforation is initially chemical peritonitis with a low bacterial load. The early injury is from acid and enzymes; infection follows if the leak is left, and is more likely when acid is suppressed.
    Traps on this card (1)
    • “Gastric perforation gives the worst infection because of the acid” → Acid causes chemical injury but keeps the bacterial count low.
    The further down the gut, the more bacteria: the stomach is nearly sterile, the colon carries 10¹¹–10¹² per mL, mostly anaerobes, so colonic perforation is the dirtiest peritonitis.

    The gut in critical illness Diarrhoea and AGI grading
    Diarrhoea is defined by number, weight and form, and is not a reason to stop feeding; the AGI grade tells you to feed, treat, search or operate.
    The concept, in order
    1. ICU diarrhoea = 3 or more loose stools a day, above 200 g/day, Bristol 5–7.
    2. First step: stop laxatives and prokinetics, review drugs and feed, test for C. difficile, and keep feeding. No antimotility agent until C. difficile is excluded.
    3. Fasting separates them: osmotic diarrhoea stops with fasting (osmotic gap above 100); secretory diarrhoea is large, plasma-like and ignores fasting (gap under 50). Gap = 290 − 2 × (Na + K).
    4. AGI (ESICM 2012): I risk (transient), II dysfunction (needs intervention), III failure (no response to intervention), IV failure with distant organ dysfunction (life-threatening).
    5. Abdominal pressure maps onto it: AGI II ≈ IAH grade I (12–15), AGI III ≈ IAH grade II (15–20), and abdominal compartment syndrome is AGI IV.

    7. Reassess 6-hourly, then 12-hourly, and watch for refeeding syndrome when feeding restarts.

    Mesenteric ischaemia Anatomy and the four causes
    Three arteries, two watersheds, four causes: each with its own patient and its own pain. Colonic ischaemia is a separate, milder disease.
    The concept, in order
    1. Coeliac: stomach, liver, proximal duodenum. SMA: distal duodenum, all the small bowel, right and proximal transverse colon. IMA: distal transverse colon to the upper rectum.
    2. The watersheds fail first in low flow: Griffiths’ point = splenic flexure, Sudeck’s point = rectosigmoid.
    3. The SMA leaves the aorta at an acute angle, so emboli lodge there, usually beyond the middle colic, sparing the proximal jejunum; a thrombosis sits at the origin.
    4. The gut tolerates a 50% fall in flow (75% for up to 12 h) by extracting more oxygen; injury starts below a MAP of 45. The mucosa dies first while the serosa still looks pink.
    5. Reperfusion adds a xanthine oxidase free-radical injury: expect high potassium, acidosis and hypotension when flow returns.
    6. Four causes: embolism (25–50%: AF, sudden pain); thrombosis (15–40%: post-prandial pain, weight loss); non-occlusive (20–25%: shock, high-dose vasopressors, cardiac surgery, dialysis), the ICU’s own and the deadliest; venous (under 10%: thrombophilia, cirrhosis, pancreatitis; pain waxing and waning).
    7. The sign of all four: pain out of proportion to a soft abdomen. Peritonism means infarction.
    8. Colonic ischaemia is different: older (90% over 60), not very sick, mild left-sided tenderness and bloody diarrhoea within 24 h, and the test is colonoscopy. Aortic surgery causes ischaemic colitis, not NOMI.

    More pairs from the cards (10)
    MES-01 Embolic vs thrombotic SMA occlusion at laparotomy
    Embolus: proximal jejunum and transverse colon spared. Thrombosis: ischaemia from the first jejunal loop onwards.
    MES-02 Ischaemic colitis vs pseudomembranous colitis at endoscopy
    Ischaemia: segmental, pale or dusky mucosa with linear ulcers at a watershed, rectum spared. C. difficile: raised yellow-white plaques, often throughout the colon.
    MES-02 Ischaemic colitis vs Crohn’s or infective proctitis
    Crohn’s: chronic, skip lesions, aphthous and deep ulcers, often terminal ileum. Proctitis: the rectum itself, which ischaemia spares.
    MES-03 Mucosa vs serosa
    The villus tip is hypoxic first: arterioles and venules run side by side in the villus, so oxygen shunts across before reaching the tip (counter-current exchange). The serosa survives longest, which is why the outside of the bowel can look viable while the mucosa is dead.
    MES-04 Reperfusion syndrome vs ongoing infarction
    Reperfusion: abrupt hyperkalaemia, acidosis and hypotension at the moment flow returns. Ongoing infarction: lactate and sepsis keep rising over hours; look for dead bowel at the second look.
    MES-05 Acute mesenteric vs colonic ischaemia
    AMI: small bowel, a clear precipitant, severely ill, CTA. Colonic: elderly, usually no precipitant, bloody diarrhoea, colonoscopy (MES-08).
    MES-06 AMI vs perforated ulcer, pancreatitis or ruptured aneurysm
    Perforation and pancreatitis give guarding early; a ruptured AAA gives back pain, a mass and collapse. AMI gives severe pain with a soft abdomen.
    MES-06 NOMI vs embolism in a septic patient
    Embolism needs a source (AF, new irregular rhythm) and starts suddenly; NOMI follows high-dose vasopressors or low flow, often after the shock has settled.
    MES-07 Early vs late AMI
    Early: pain out of proportion, soft abdomen. Late: guarding, rigidity, shock — transmural infarction; peritonism means dead bowel.
    MES-08 Left-sided vs isolated right colon ischaemia
    Right-sided colonic ischaemia shares the SMA supply and may herald AMI; ACG says do CT angiography and treat it as more severe.

    Mesenteric ischaemia Diagnosis and treatment
    No blood test rules it in or out: suspicion goes straight to biphasic CT angiography, peritonitis straight to theatre. Restore flow by the cause, resect only dead bowel, look again.
    The concept, in order
    1. No blood test confirms or excludes AMI: D-dimer is sensitive (96%) but non-specific; I-FABP is specific (93%) but insensitive; lactate is late, a sign of transmural infarction.
    2. Biphasic CT angiography is the test (sensitivity 94%, specificity 95%): no oral contrast, and don’t withhold contrast for AKI.
    3. CT signs: arterial filling defect, non-enhancing or thick wall, dilatation; pneumatosis with portal venous gas = dead bowel.
    4. Peritonitis → laparotomy without imaging. A normal CT with high suspicion → catheter angiography (especially for NOMI).
    5. Tonometry reads the mucosa’s CO₂: a widening gastric-to-arterial PCO₂ gap (normal under 7 mmHg) means gut ischaemia. The card calls it the only valid test, but it is rarely used.
    6. General care: fluids, NG decompression, broad-spectrum antibiotics, weight-based unfractionated heparin. Avoid vasopressin, high-dose noradrenaline and digoxin; if an inotrope is needed, use dobutamine, low-dose dopamine or milrinone.

    Abdominal pressure Measure and define
    Abdominal pressure is a number from the bladder: 12 is hypertension, and over 20 with a new failing organ is compartment syndrome.
    The concept, in order
    1. Normal IAP in the critically ill is 5–7 mmHg; obesity and pregnancy raise the baseline without organ effects.
    2. Measure through the bladder (the gold standard), every 4 h while at risk: supine, zero at the mid-axillary line, instil at most 25 mL of saline, wait 30–60 s, read at end-expiration with a relaxed abdominal wall.
    3. Every departure pushes the reading up: more saline, head up, abdominal muscles contracting.
    4. IAH = sustained or repeated IAP of 12 or more: grade I 12–15, II 16–20, III 21–25, IV above 25.
    5. ACS = sustained IAP above 20 with new organ dysfunction. It is not a grade: even grade IV without organ failure is only IAH.
    6. APP = MAP − IAP (target 60 or more). The kidney feels IAP twice, filtration gradient = MAP − 2 × IAP, so it fails first.

    Abdominal pressure Causes, effects and management
    Pressure rises when the abdomen holds more or gives less. It lowers output while CVP and airway pressures read high. Treat medically first; decompress refractory compartment syndrome.
    The concept, in order
    1. Primary ACS starts in the abdomen or pelvis (trauma, damage-control laparotomy, packs): early surgery or radiology. Secondary comes from massive resuscitation elsewhere (burns, sepsis): medical first. Recurrent returns after treatment of either.
    2. Four groups of cause: less wall compliance (burn eschar, tight closure), more intraluminal contents (ileus, gastric distension), more abdominal contents (ascites, blood, packs), capillary leak with fluid resuscitation. In trauma, 3 L or more of crystalloid predicts both types.
    3. Effects: venous return and cardiac output fall; the chest stiffens (high peak and plateau pressures); oliguria at 15, anuria at 30 mmHg; gut flow falls and oedema raises the pressure further; ICP rises.
    4. The numbers that rise mislead: about half of IAP reaches the chest, so CVP and wedge read falsely high. Transmural CVP = CVP − 0.5 × IAP, or use volumetric indices.
    5. Medical steps from IAP 12: relax the wall (sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular block; head of bed ≤ 30°), empty the gut (NG, rectal tube, prokinetics), drain collections (paracentesis, percutaneous), stop the positive balance (diuretics, ultrafiltration).
    6. ACS: find the cause. A perforation or bleed is opened now; fluid-driven disease is drained and dried first; decompressive laparotomy with temporary closure if organ failure persists (usually grade III–IV).
    7. Stop measuring when IAP stays under 12. Protocolised WSACS care raised survival from 50% to 72%. Expect reperfusion (hypotension, high potassium, acidosis) on decompression.

    a Improve wall compliance: sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade; avoid head of bed over 30 degrees.
    b Evacuate intraluminal contents: nasogastric and rectal decompression, prokinetics.
    c Evacuate abdominal collections: paracentesis, percutaneous drainage.
    d Correct positive fluid balance: avoid excess fluid; diuretics, colloid, ultrafiltration.
    e Organ support: optimise ventilation; use transmural pressures.
    Watch: Start at IAP of 12 or more. Enteral feed is minimised while pressure is high.
    Why, part by part
    • a Improve wall compliance: sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade; avoid head of bed over 30 degrees. Pain and agitation tighten the abdominal wall; WSACS suggests analgesia and sedation and a brief trial of neuromuscular blockade as a temporising measure (2D). Paralysis can lower IAP by several mmHg within minutes, which buys time in grade II–III IAH. Remove tight dressings; an abdominal eschar needs escharotomy.
    • b Evacuate intraluminal contents: nasogastric and rectal decompression, prokinetics. WSACS suggests liberal NG and rectal decompression when the stomach or colon is dilated, and neostigmine for refractory colonic ileus (2D). This is the first, least invasive step (the Marrow post-splenectomy question).
    • c Evacuate abdominal collections: paracentesis, percutaneous drainage. Percutaneous catheter drainage is suggested over doing nothing (2C) and even over immediate decompressive laparotomy (2D) when there is obvious free fluid, for example ascites in pancreatitis or burns. It is therapeutic, not just diagnostic, and is not contraindicated in ACS.
    • d Correct positive fluid balance: avoid excess fluid; diuretics, colloid, ultrafiltration. WSACS suggests a protocol to avoid a positive cumulative balance after resuscitation (2C) and a high plasma:red cell ratio in massive haemorrhage (2D). It makes no recommendation on diuretics, albumin or RRT, so furosemide is not the answer for an oliguric patient whose kidneys are compressed.
    • e Organ support: optimise ventilation; use transmural pressures. Set PEEP to counter the pressure on the lung bases, interpret CVP and plateau transmurally (IAH-09), and aim for APP of at least 60 (an observational target; WSACS 2013 makes no recommendation on APP).
    Traps on this card (3)
    • “IAP 19 after laparotomy, poor compliance, falling urine: surgical decompression” → 19 is grade II, not ACS; a brief trial of neuromuscular blockade is the keyed answer (only 34% right). Furosemide does not treat compression, and antibiotics treat nothing here.
    • “Percutaneous drainage is only diagnostic” or “contraindicated in ACS” → It is a valid non-surgical decompression (77% right).
    • “Immediate laparotomy for septic free fluid with organ dysfunction” → If the fluid can be drained, ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage comes first (72% right).
    Before anyone opens the abdomen, relax the wall, empty the gut, drain the fluid and stop the positive balance; each lowers IAP by removing a cause.

    Before the card: Step 4 is what the pressure does. Three questions: the kidney mechanism (88% right), an ‘all except’ in which a third missed that airway pressures rise, and the Marrow question on the lung in a ventilated patient.
    End-organ effects:
    a Heart: venous return falls (caval compression); raised diaphragm impairs ventricular compliance; CVP and wedge pressure read falsely high.
    b Lung: low compliance and FRC, atelectasis: hypoxia, hypercapnia, high peak and plateau pressures.
    c Kidney: oliguria at 15 mmHg; anuria at 30 mmHg.
    d Gut: mesenteric and mucosal flow fall; venous stasis and oedema raise the pressure further; translocation.
    e Brain: raised intrathoracic pressure impedes venous drainage: ICP rises.
    Watch: The gut effect is a vicious circle: oedema raises pressure, pressure causes more oedema.
    Why, part by part
    • a Heart: venous return falls (caval compression); raised diaphragm impairs ventricular compliance; CVP and wedge pressure read falsely high. Preload falls and afterload rises (compressed vascular beds raise systemic resistance), so cardiac output falls. The pressure transmitted to the chest raises CVP even though the ventricle is underfilled (IAH-09).
    • b Lung: low compliance and FRC, atelectasis: hypoxia, hypercapnia, high peak and plateau pressures. The diaphragm is pushed up, the chest wall becomes stiff, and the respiratory system compliance falls, so the same tidal volume needs higher pressure. Ventilation pressures rise, not fall.
    • c Kidney: oliguria at 15 mmHg; anuria at 30 mmHg. Renal vein compression, direct parenchymal compression and the filtration gradient (MAP − 2 × IAP, IAH-04) reduce filtration; low cardiac output and renin–angiotensin activation add to it.
    • d Gut: mesenteric and mucosal flow fall; venous stasis and oedema raise the pressure further; translocation. Gut perfusion falls early, at moderate pressures, often before urine output falls: mucosal acidosis and oedema add volume and raise IAP more, so the problem feeds itself, and bacterial translocation drives sepsis and multi-organ failure. The liver loses arterial and portal flow, so lactate clearance falls.
    • e Brain: raised intrathoracic pressure impedes venous drainage: ICP rises. Jugular venous outflow is impeded; decompressive laparotomy has lowered refractory intracranial hypertension in head-injured patients with IAH.
    Traps on this card (2)
    • “Raised IAP decreases ventilation pressure” → Peak and plateau pressures rise; this is the exception in the GT7 ‘all except’ (66% right).
    • “Oliguria in ACS is from low contractility or ATN” → It is the pressure (renal vein and parenchymal compression, filtration gradient).
    Raised abdominal pressure lowers cardiac output, urine and gut flow while it raises CVP, airway pressure and ICP; the numbers that rise are the ones that mislead.

    IAH-11 How is the open abdomen managed after decompression?
    Negative-pressure wound therapy is the recommended temporary closure (WSACS 1C); IAP is still measured because recurrent ACS can occur with an open abdomen; the abdomen is re-explored every 24–48 h (WSES 2018) and the fascia closed during the same admission as soon as physiology allows, early closure meaning within about 4–7 days, because closure rates fall and fistula, infection and ‘frozen abdomen’ rise with each day.
