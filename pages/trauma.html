The first hour: who dies, triage, scores, the surveys

Before you start

Trauma care rests on one principle: treat the injury that will kill first before the diagnosis is complete. The primary survey (xABCDE) finds and treats immediate threats; the secondary survey is the full head-to-toe examination and history; the tertiary survey, within 24 hours, catches what both missed. Shock in trauma is bleeding until proven otherwise, and the systolic pressure is a late sign because vasoconstriction holds it until about a third of the blood volume has gone. Severity is described two ways: anatomically (how bad the injuries are) and physiologically (how deranged the patient is).

  1. Who dies from trauma, and when. Bleeding and brain injury kill most trauma patients, and most of those deaths happen in the first hours, which is why the first hour of care is organised so rigidly.
  2. Defining and triaging the injured. Polytrauma means serious injury in two regions plus a physiological failure, and field triage sends the patient with deranged vital signs or a high-risk injury pattern to the highest-level centre.
  3. Scoring the damage. Anatomical scores (AIS, ISS, NISS) count the injuries, physiological scores (RTS) measure the derangement, and TRISS combines them to compare outcomes between centres, not to triage.
  4. Mechanism predicts the injuries. The way the energy was delivered tells you which injuries to look for before the examination finds them.
  5. The primary survey. xABCDE treats each threat to life as it is found, in the order in which it would kill: catastrophic bleeding, airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure.
  6. Grading and resuscitating shock. Haemorrhagic shock is graded by how much blood has gone, and the shocked patient gets blood, not crystalloid, and has the circulation filled before the airway is taken.

Bleeding and blood: the triad, damage control, transfusion, drugs

Before you start

Haemorrhage is the commonest preventable cause of death after injury, and most of those deaths happen in the first few hours. A clot needs enough fibrinogen, platelets, ionised calcium, working enzymes (which need a normal temperature and pH) and an endothelium that is not being broken down. Shock alone disturbs all of these, which is why the bleeding trauma patient is treated as a coagulopathy from the start, not just as a volume problem. SPECIAL05A covered definitions, scores, the primary survey and the ATLS shock classes; this deck covers what happens to blood and physiology and how they are rescued.

  1. Shock feeds the lethal triad. Hypoperfusion makes the patient cold and acidotic, cold and acid stop clots forming, and the bleeding that follows deepens the shock, so lactate and base deficit measure how far down that spiral he already is.
  2. Coagulopathy begins before any fluid. A quarter of the badly injured arrive coagulopathic because shock activates protein C and fibrinolysis and consumes fibrinogen; dilution, cold and acid add the late, iatrogenic layer.
  3. Damage control resuscitation. When physiology is exhausted, keep the pressure low but adequate, give blood products instead of crystalloid in about a 1:1:1 ratio, and choose the patient for a short operation, not a complete one.
  4. Stopping the bleeding mechanically. Bleeding stops only with pressure, a binder, a tourniquet, packing, embolisation or an abbreviated operation; REBOA is a temporary bridge with doubtful benefit.
  5. Blood in ratio, to targets. Massive transfusion starts on a bedside score with group O cells and AB plasma, runs in a fixed ratio, then switches to laboratory and viscoelastic targets, with fibrinogen the first factor to replace.
  6. Drugs, guideline and the sweep. Tranexamic acid within 3 hours, calcium kept normal and rFVIIa only as a last resort complete the bundle that the European guideline sets out.

It needs the ISS, which is not known until imaging or surgery is done, so it cannot be used in the field.

    Why, part by part
    • a AIS: severity of each injury, 1 minor to 6 unsurvivable. 1 minor, 2 moderate, 3 serious, 4 severe, 5 critical, 6 unsurvivable with current treatment. It describes one injury and says nothing about the patient as a whole.
    • b ISS: sum of the squares of the highest AIS in the 3 most injured body regions; range 0–75 (any AIS 6 → 75); over 15 = major trauma. Squaring makes the score rise steeply with severity, matching the steep rise in mortality. Only one injury per region counts, so two serious injuries in the same region contribute only the worse one. An injured patient scores at least 1; 0 means no injury.

A gunshot that injures liver, bowel and kidney, or a head with a subdural, contusion and a skull fracture, puts several serious injuries in one region. ISS ignores all but one of them; NISS counts them, so it predicts mortality better in exactly those patients.

Oxygen and basic manoeuvres buy minutes while the circulation is filled. An expanding neck haematoma, an inhalational burn or a failing airway cannot wait. (4) Shock: blood products and TXA, bind the pelvis, decompress the chest, treat tamponade; vasopressors if neurogenic shock. The paralytic dose is not reduced (and may be increased), because slow circulation delays its onset.     • b Stable or transient responder with solid-organ injury, any grade (e.g. grade III spleen or liver with a blush) → non-operative in ICU/HDU + IR angioembolisation; NOM succeeds in over 80–90%; avoid large crystalloid boluses; vaccinate after splenectomy or major splenic embolisation. The leg’s anterior compartment is tight and the tibia is the commonest fracture behind it. Compartment pressure needed to stop capillary flow is far below arterial pressure, so the distal pulse stays present while the muscle is dying; waiting for pulselessness means waiting for necrosis.
    • b Remove all circumferential dressings and splints at once. A cast, bandage or splint adds external pressure to the compartment; splitting the cast down to the skin can lower pressure markedly. The limb is kept at heart level, not elevated, because elevation lowers the arterial pressure driving flow.
    • c Unreliable examination (sedated, obtunded) → measure: delta-P = DIASTOLIC BP − compartment pressure; an absolute pressure over 30 alone ignores perfusion; measurement is an adjunct, not infallible. Perfusion depends on the gradient, so a pressure of 30 is dangerous in a hypotensive patient and tolerable in a hypertensive one. In the ICU patient who cannot report pain, measurement (or repeated measurement) replaces the history.
    • d Not relieved, or delta-P under 30 mmHg → emergency fasciotomy (ideally within 6 hours). Muscle tolerates about 4–6 hours of ischaemia before irreversible necrosis. Fasciotomy opens all compartments (four in the leg), and the wounds are left open. Afterwards, watch for rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia.

    Why, part by part
    • a Restricted volume replacement to a target SBP of 80–90 mmHg until bleeding is controlled (no TBI). Grade 1B. This is a little higher than the 70–90 often taught (POLY-15).
    • b Severe TBI (GCS 8 or less): MAP 80 mmHg or more. Grade 1C. Cerebral perfusion follows arterial pressure once autoregulation is lost.
    • c Vasopressors with fluids for life-threatening hypotension. The guideline names noradrenaline, added when fluids alone do not reach the target, and dobutamine if the myocardium is failing. A vasopressor reduces the fluid needed to hold the pressure.
    • d Isotonic crystalloid; avoid excess 0.9% saline. The 6th edition recommends starting with 0.9% saline or a balanced crystalloid (Grade 1B); its rationale adds that if saline is used it should be limited to about 1–1.5 L, because large volumes cause hyperchloraemic acidosis. Hypotonic solutions such as Ringer’s lactate are avoided in severe head injury.
    • e Haemoglobin 7–9 g/dL. Grade 1C. Higher targets add risk without benefit.
    • f Start coagulation support with plasma:RBC at least 1:2, or fibrinogen concentrate plus RBC. Either a plasma-based or a factor-concentrate strategy is accepted (Grade 1C), with a high platelet:RBC ratio; then goal-directed treatment guided by viscoelastic or laboratory tests.
    • g TXA 1 g over 10 minutes then 1 g over 8 hours, within 3 hours. Grade 1A, the strongest recommendation in the guideline: as soon as possible, en route if feasible, and not waiting for viscoelastic results.
    • h Platelets over 50; over 100 with ongoing bleeding or TBI. In the 6th edition the higher threshold of 100 × 10⁹/L applies to TBI; over 50 is the target with ongoing bleeding.
