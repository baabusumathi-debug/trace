Obstetrics · NEET-SS, with tricks

OBS01 · Physiology, recognition, airway, organ support

Before you startThe fetus has no oxygen supply of its own: what reaches it is maternal arterial oxygen content multiplied by uterine blood flow, and the uterine vessels are already fully dilated and cannot autoregulate, so any fall in maternal blood pressure, cardiac output or saturation is passed straight on. Fetal carbon dioxide leaves by diffusing down a gradient into maternal blood, which is why the mother normally runs a low PaCO₂. From about 20 weeks the uterus is large enough to press on the inferior vena cava and aorta when she lies flat. Treating the mother well is the treatment of the fetus; almost nothing in this deck asks you to trade one against the other.

OBS02A · Hypertensive disorders, eclampsia, HELLP and fatty liver

Before you startNormal pregnancy lowers blood pressure in the first half, because systemic vascular resistance falls, and raises plasma volume by almost half, so a pressure of 140/90 in the second half is abnormal for a pregnant woman even though it looks mild. The spiral arteries that feed the placenta are normally remodelled into wide, low-resistance channels by about 20 weeks, which is why 20 weeks is the dividing line in every definition in this deck. The placenta has no autoregulation, so its flow follows maternal pressure; this is why blood pressure is lowered in a controlled way and never to normal. Doses and cut-offs here follow ACOG’s 2020 bulletin, which the examiners’ keys use, with ISSHP and NICE where they differ.

OBS02B · Haemorrhage, AFE, sepsis, heart, arrest, OHSS

Before you startBy term the mother’s blood volume has risen by about 45% and her cardiac output by 30–50%, and the uterus receives roughly 700 mL of blood a minute, so obstetric bleeding is fast but her pulse and pressure hide it for a long time. Pregnancy is also a clotting state: fibrinogen doubles to 4–6 g/L, so a ‘normal’ adult value is low for her. Lying flat after 20 weeks, the uterus presses on the vena cava and aorta and cuts venous return, which matters in every collapse. OBS02A covered the physiology and hypertensive disease; this deck covers the emergencies in which she bleeds, collapses or arrests.

    OBS01 · 1 Who gets sick, and why
    Pregnant and postpartum women are a small share of ICU admissions, the causes are either diseases of pregnancy itself or ordinary diseases made worse by it, and most of the deaths are preventable.
    1. Obstetric patients are 1–4.5% of ICU admissions; mortality 5–20%; 70% of maternal deaths are preventable.
    2. Commonest reason for ICU: pre-eclampsia/eclampsia (~75%), then medical disease, then haemorrhage.
    3. Diseases of pregnancy: pre-eclampsia, HELLP, fatty liver, amniotic fluid embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, obstetric haemorrhage, chorioamnionitis/septic abortion, magnesium toxicity, tocolytic pulmonary oedema, OHSS.
    4. Diseases worsened by pregnancy: ARDS, aspiration, pulmonary embolism, urosepsis, hepatitis E, varicella pneumonia, falciparum malaria, H1N1, cerebral venous thrombosis, DKA, valve disease, pulmonary hypertension.
    5. Why it is hard: altered physiology, pregnancy-specific diseases, the fetus, and clinicians' unfamiliarity.
    6. The obstetric lethal triad is hypotension, hypoxia, anaemia: the three determinants of oxygen delivery to mother and fetus.
    7. Heart disease (top-up): NYHA III–IV mothers are ~10% of cardiac patients but ~85% of cardiac deaths; pregnancy is contraindicated in PAH, EF <30%, severe MS, symptomatic AS, Marfan aorta >45 mm.

    OBS-02 HELLP vs acute fatty liver of pregnancy vs TTP/HUS
    HELLP: hypertension, haemolysis, platelets down, transaminases up, glucose normal. Acute fatty liver: liver failure picture with hypoglycaemia, raised ammonia, prolonged PT, low fibrinogen. TTP/HUS: haemolysis and low platelets with normal clotting, and it does not remit after delivery.
    OBS-02 Amniotic fluid embolism vs pulmonary embolism
    Both give sudden collapse and hypoxia around delivery. Amniotic fluid embolism adds early DIC and bleeding, which may be the presenting feature; pulmonary embolism does not cause a consumptive coagulopathy.
    OBS-01 “Haemorrhage is the commonest reason for obstetric ICU admission”
    On this card’s chart hypertensive disease (pre-eclampsia and eclampsia) is far ahead at 75%, with medical disease 14% and haemorrhage 11%. The proportions vary by series; in many high-income series haemorrhage and hypertension are closer together.
    OBS-02 “Viral hepatitis behaves in pregnancy as in other adults”
    Hepatitis E is the exception: it causes fulminant hepatic failure with a high maternal mortality, mainly in the third trimester.
    OBS-02 “Peripartum cardiomyopathy is a pre-existing heart disease”
    It is listed with the obstetric causes: new left ventricular systolic failure towards the end of pregnancy or in the months after delivery with no other cause. Valvular, congenital and pulmonary hypertensive disease are the pre-existing group.

    OBS01 · 2 The pregnant body’s new normal
    By term cardiac output and blood volume are up by nearly half, resistance and diastolic pressure are down, she hyperventilates to a PaCO₂ of 30 with a bicarbonate of 20, and creatinine and haemoglobin are low, so ‘normal’ adult values can mean disease.
    1. Heart: cardiac output +40–50% (stroke volume first, then heart rate +15–25%); blood volume +45%; SVR −20%; diastolic falls mid-pregnancy; CVP unchanged; O₂ consumption +30–40%.
    2. Lung: tidal volume +40%, FRC −20%, vital capacity unchanged; progesterone drives the extra ventilation.
    3. Gas: pH 7.40–7.47, PaCO₂ ~30, HCO₃ 18–22: a compensated respiratory alkalosis. A PaCO₂ of 40 in a breathless woman means she is tiring, and the low bicarbonate leaves less buffer.
    4. Blood: plasma rises more than red cells → dilutional Hb ~10.5–11 with normal MCV and ferritin. Fibrinogen 4–6 g/L: hypercoagulable, so ≤2 g/L is already DIC.
    5. Kidney: GFR +50%, creatinine 0.4–0.8 → a creatinine of 1.0 is abnormal.
    6. Gut: lax lower oesophageal sphincter and slow transit → aspiration risk.
    7. Aortocaval compression from ~20 weeks: lying flat, the uterus squeezes the IVC (less preload) and aorta → hypotension and poor placental flow. Fix: left uterine displacement, a wedge under the right hip; manual displacement in arrest.
    8. Heart sounds: S3, loud split S1 and a soft ejection murmur are normal; any diastolic murmur, fixed split S2 or S4 is disease.

    More pairs from the cards (7)
    OBS-03 Normal pregnancy vs early sepsis on the monitor
    Both have a high output, low resistance, a fast pulse and warm peripheries. Sepsis adds fever, a respiratory rate of 25 or more, systolic 90 or less, raised lactate or altered mentation (OBS-10).
    OBS-03 Physiological S3 and flow murmur vs heart disease
    Normal: S3, loud split S1, a soft (grade 1–2) ejection systolic murmur, mild ankle oedema.
    Disease: any diastolic murmur, a fixed split S2, a loud systolic murmur, a fourth sound, or symptoms at rest.
    OBS-04 Physiological breathlessness of pregnancy vs disease
    Physiological: an awareness of breathing from early pregnancy, at rest or on talking, with a normal rate, saturation and chest.
    Disease: rate 25 or more, saturation under 95%, sudden onset, orthopnoea, or a PaCO₂ that has risen to 40.
    OBS-05 Normal pregnancy gas vs a mixed disorder
    Normal: pH 7.44, PaCO₂ 30, bicarbonate 20. If PaCO₂ is 30 with a pH of 7.30 and bicarbonate 14, there is an added metabolic acidosis. If PaCO₂ is 40 with a pH of 7.36, there is relative hypoventilation.
    OBS-06 Supine hypotension vs haemorrhage in a pregnant trauma patient
    Both give hypotension and tachycardia on the table. Displacement of the uterus restores pressure within a minute in aortocaval compression; if it does not, look for bleeding. Displace first, then investigate.
    OBS-07 Dilutional anaemia vs iron deficiency in pregnancy
    Dilutional: haemoglobin about 10.5–11 g/dL, normal MCV and ferritin.
    Iron deficiency: lower haemoglobin, low MCV and ferritin.
    OBS-07 Normal pregnancy coagulation vs early DIC
    Normal: fibrinogen 4–6 g/L, platelets normal or slightly low.
    Early DIC (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, haemorrhage): fibrinogen falling to 2 g/L or less, which a non-pregnant reference range would still call normal.
    OBS-03 “Cardiac output in labour rises 30–50%” 41% right
    That is the antenatal figure on the card. DocT keys 60–80% for labour (only 41% right); most chose the antenatal range. Standard texts put about +50% in the second stage and the 60–80% peak immediately after delivery, so when a stem says labour or just delivered, pick the highest range offered.
    OBS-03 “Loud S1” as the heart sound of pregnancy
    A loud, widely split S1 does occur, but the keyed answer is the third heart sound, from rapid filling of a volume-loaded ventricle (50% right). The two options that are never normal are a diastolic murmur and a fixed split S2.
    OBS-03 “Only progesterone lowers vascular resistance”
    Progesterone, relaxin and nitric oxide all contribute; ‘all of the above’ is keyed (82%).
    OBS-03 “Blood pressure rises in pregnancy because volume rises 45%”
    The volume fills a dilated circuit; pressure falls in mid-pregnancy and only returns to baseline at term. Of cardiac output, stroke volume, heart rate and blood pressure, only blood pressure decreases (84%).
    OBS-03 “The diastolic dip is due to increased cardiac output or heart rate”
    A higher output would raise pressure. Pressure falls because resistance falls more than flow rises (92% right).
    More traps (14)
    OBS-06 “Half-dose adrenaline”, “delay intubation until ROSC” or “magnesium immediately” in maternal arrest
    Drugs, doses and shock energies are unchanged; the airway is secured early because of aspiration risk; magnesium is for eclampsia, not an arrest drug (calcium is given if she was on magnesium). The critical modification is manual left uterine displacement (97% right).
    OBS-03 “A CVP of 15 or a high pulmonary vascular resistance is expected at 34 weeks”
    Filling pressures are unchanged and pulmonary resistance falls.
    OBS-04 “Vital capacity falls in pregnancy”
    Unchanged: the wider rib cage compensates for the raised diaphragm. What falls is FRC (expiratory reserve and residual volume).
    OBS-04 “Rapid desaturation is due to reduced oxygen consumption” or “to increased FRC”
    The reverse on both counts: oxygen consumption is increased and FRC is decreased (the pairing the card’s watch line cites from a Grand Test).
    OBS-04 “She breathes faster, not deeper”
    The rise in ventilation is mostly tidal volume; a clearly raised rate is a warning sign.
    OBS-05 “PaCO₂ 40 mmHg is a normal gas in a pregnant asthmatic”
    Normal for her is about 30; 40 means ventilatory failure is starting.
    OBS-05 “Bicarbonate 19 with a base deficit of 3 means metabolic acidosis in pregnancy”
    That is the normal renal compensation for chronic hyperventilation; look at pH and the anion gap before calling acidosis.
    OBS-05 “Pregnancy causes an uncompensated respiratory alkalosis”
    It is compensated: pH is only slightly raised because bicarbonate has fallen.
    OBS-06 “Tilt the whole patient during CPR”
    Tilt reduces compression force and makes airway work and defibrillation awkward; keep her supine and displace the uterus by hand.
    OBS-06 “The second rescuer is there for the perimortem caesarean”
    The NEET 2025 key is uterine displacement, needed from the first compression; perimortem delivery follows at 4–5 minutes if there is no return of circulation.
    OBS-06 “Give a fluid bolus or vasopressor first for hypotension when supine at 35 weeks”
    Reposition first; it is immediate and treats the cause.
    OBS-07 “Fibrinogen decreases in pregnancy”
    It rises; ‘decreased fibrinogen’ is the option that is NOT a physiological change. Reduced FRC, increased cardiac output and increased GFR are all true.
    OBS-07 “Anaemia of pregnancy is due to increased red cell mass, increased oxygen consumption or low resistance”
    Red cell mass does increase, which would raise haemoglobin; the concentration falls because plasma volume expands more.
    OBS-07 “A creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL is normal in pregnancy”
    Above about 0.8 mg/dL is abnormal and should prompt a search for pre-eclampsia, sepsis or obstruction.

    Why, part by part
    • a Cardiac output up 40–50%; stroke volume up 30%; heart rate up 15–25%. Output starts rising in the first trimester and is near its plateau by about 28–32 weeks. Stroke volume does the early work as volume expands and afterload falls; heart rate goes on climbing to term (about 10–20 beats/min above baseline), as the watch line says. These are antenatal figures at rest. In labour each contraction squeezes 300–500 mL from the uterus into the circulation and pain adds sympathetic drive, so output rises further, and immediately after delivery it peaks at 60–80% above the pre-pregnancy value as the emptied uterus returns its blood and the vena cava is released. That is when a woman with mitral stenosis or pulmonary hypertension goes into pulmonary oedema.
    • b Intravascular volume up 45%. Oestrogen and the fall in resistance activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and retain sodium and water. Plasma expands more than red cells (OBS-07). The extra volume fills a dilated circuit, which is why filling pressures do not rise, and it is a reserve against blood loss at delivery: she can lose well over a litre before the pulse and pressure change.
    • c Systemic vascular resistance down 20%; systolic BP minimal change; diastolic BP down 20% at mid-pregnancy, back to baseline at term. Progesterone relaxes vascular smooth muscle and blunts the response to angiotensin II, relaxin and endothelial nitric oxide add to it, and the placenta is a low-resistance circuit in parallel. Diastolic pressure is the pressure most dependent on resistance, so it falls most, with its lowest point in the second trimester; the pulse pressure widens. A pressure of 120/80 at 24 weeks is therefore relatively high. On a pulmonary artery catheter at term, expect an output of about 6–6.5 L/min and an SVR of roughly 900–1200 dyn·s/cm⁵ (Clark 1989: 6.2 L/min and 1210, against 4.3 and 1530 post partum).
    • d CVP unchanged. The larger volume sits in a more compliant venous bed and a dilated heart, so central venous and wedge pressures stay in the non-pregnant range, and pulmonary vascular resistance falls along with systemic. A CVP of 15 mmHg or a raised pulmonary resistance is disease, not pregnancy.
    • e Oxygen consumption up 30–40%. The fetus, placenta, uterus and the extra cardiac and respiratory work all add to it; published figures at term range from about 20% to 40%, and it rises again in labour. Together with the fall in FRC (OBS-04) this explains rapid desaturation at intubation (OBS-08).

    Why, part by part
    • a Plasma volume rises more than red cell mass: dilutional anaemia. Plasma expands by about 45–50% and red cell mass by only 20–30%, so haemoglobin concentration falls although the total amount of haemoglobin has gone up. A haemoglobin of 10.5–11 g/dL with normal iron studies is physiological. Clotting moves the other way: fibrinogen rises (to about 4–6 g/L) with factors VII, VIII, X and von Willebrand factor, and protein S falls. Pregnancy is hypercoagulable, which protects against bleeding at delivery and raises the risk of thrombosis, and it means a fibrinogen of 2 g/L during postpartum haemorrhage is already low and predicts severe bleeding.
    • b GFR and renal plasma flow rise by about half; creatinine falls (to about 0.4–0.8 mg/dL). Renal vasodilatation and the higher cardiac output increase filtration, so creatinine and urea fall. A creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL, normal on the laboratory report, can represent the loss of a large fraction of her GFR. Renally cleared drugs are eliminated faster. The ureters and renal pelves dilate, more on the right, which predisposes to pyelonephritis (OBS-02).
    • c Lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes (progesterone); slow transit; constipation. Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle throughout the gut, and the uterus displaces the stomach and raises intragastric pressure, so the barrier pressure across the sphincter falls. Gastric emptying itself is close to normal until labour, when pain and opioids slow it markedly.
    • d Aspiration: full abdomen, lax sphincter, slow transit. She is treated as having a full stomach from mid-pregnancy: rapid sequence induction with a cuffed tube, antacid prophylaxis before anaesthesia, and caution with non-invasive ventilation when consciousness is reduced (OBS-08, OBS-12).
    • e A high-calorie state; needs rise in labour and are highest in lactation. Energy needs rise by roughly 300 kcal/day in later pregnancy and about 500 kcal/day in lactation. Fasting leads to ketosis sooner than in a non-pregnant woman, so prolonged starvation in a critically ill pregnant woman is avoided.

    Why, part by part
    • a The gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava (less venous return) and the aorta (more afterload). Lying supine at term the vena cava can be almost completely occluded; blood returns through the azygos and paravertebral veins. Most women compensate by vasoconstriction and a faster pulse, which is why only a minority feel faint, but the compensation is lost under anaesthesia, with hypovolaemia or sepsis, and in cardiac arrest.
    • b Blood pressure and cardiac output fall; uteroplacental perfusion is compromised. Preload falls, so output can drop by a quarter or more. Uterine flow suffers twice: lower maternal output, and aortic compression lowering pressure in the uterine arteries, so the fetal heart trace can deteriorate even when the pressure measured in her arm looks acceptable.
    • c Left uterine displacement: a wedge under the right hip, or manual displacement of the uterus to the left. The vena cava lies to the right of the spine, so the uterus is moved to the left: a 15° left lateral tilt, a wedge under the right hip, or a hand pushing or pulling the uterus leftwards. In cardiac arrest the method is manual displacement with the woman flat on a firm surface, because a tilted chest makes compressions less effective; this is the job of the second rescuer.
    • d From about 20 weeks (uterus at the umbilicus). If gestation is unknown, a uterus palpable at or above the umbilicus is the trigger. The same landmark defines who is considered for perimortem caesarean delivery: if circulation has not returned by about 4 minutes of correct resuscitation, the uterus is emptied (aiming for delivery by 5 minutes) to relieve the compression and help the mother’s resuscitation.

    Why, part by part
    • a 1. Prevent: active management of the third stage (oxytocin at delivery, controlled cord traction, uterine massage). A uterotonic given as the baby is born (oxytocin 10 IU is the standard) makes the uterus contract as the placenta separates, and it carries almost all of the benefit. Controlled cord traction is done by a skilled attendant. Early cord clamping is no longer part of the package: the cord is clamped after 1–3 minutes.
    • b 2. Resuscitate: two large cannulae, oxygen, monitoring, bloods and cross-match. Resuscitation runs alongside every later rung. Read the response to the first fluid bolus as in trauma: a woman whose pressure rises and then falls again within minutes with a rising pulse is a transient responder, which means ongoing bleeding of roughly 15–40% of her blood volume; she needs blood and control of the source, not observation and not vasopressors alone.
    • c 3. Bimanual massage and oxytocin; find which T. Compressing the uterus between a hand on the abdomen and a fist in the vagina squeezes the placental bed while the drug works. At the same time examine for tears, check the placenta and look at whether the blood is clotting (OBS-31).
    • d 4. Second uterotonic: carboprost, methylergonovine or misoprostol. Chosen by asthma and blood pressure (OBS-32). Tranexamic acid belongs here too (the card’s watch line): 1 g IV as soon as PPH is diagnosed and within 3 hours of birth, repeated once if bleeding continues after 30 minutes. In the WOMAN trial (20,060 women) it cut death from bleeding from 1.9% to 1.5%, and from 1.7% to 1.2% when given within 3 hours; after 3 hours there was no benefit, and it did not reduce hysterectomy. Bleeding activates fibrinolysis early, so an antifibrinolytic works early or not at all.
    • e 5. Massive: transfuse red cells, platelets and factors; vasopressors. Activate the massive transfusion protocol with balanced products and early fibrinogen (OBS-34). Vasopressors only bridge while volume is restored; they do not replace blood.
    • f 6. Tamponade or packing; compression (B-Lynch) sutures; vessel ligation or embolisation. An intrauterine balloon presses on the placental bed from inside and is the first mechanical step for atony that has not responded to drugs; it controls bleeding in about 85% of reported cases. It does not work for a ruptured uterus or a lacerated artery. If the abdomen is open, a compression suture folds and squeezes the atonic uterus; stepwise devascularisation follows (uterine arteries, then utero-ovarian vessels, then the internal iliac arteries, which is technically difficult and less reliable: about 40–60% in the older series of internal iliac ligation alone, against about 85% when all ligation and devascularisation procedures are pooled). Embolisation by interventional radiology suits the woman who is still bleeding but haemodynamically stable enough to be moved; it succeeds in about 90% of reported cases and preserves the uterus.
    • g 7. Hysterectomy. The definitive step when the others fail or when she is too unstable to try them. It is done sooner, without climbing the uterus-sparing rungs, in placenta accreta spectrum: the placenta has invaded the uterine wall and peeling it off tears the bed open, so the planned operation is caesarean hysterectomy with the placenta left in place.

    Why, part by part
    • a Continuous manual left uterine displacement. From about 20 weeks, when the fundus is at or above the umbilicus, the supine uterus compresses the vena cava and aorta and cuts venous return, so chest compressions generate little output. One rescuer pushes or pulls the uterus to the mother’s left with the hands throughout. Manual displacement keeps her flat on a firm surface; tilting the whole patient makes compressions less effective.
    • b IV access above the diaphragm. Drugs and fluid given into a leg or femoral vein must pass the compressed vena cava and may not reach the heart. Use an arm, neck or humeral intraosseous site.
    • c Expect a difficult airway: most experienced operator; 100% oxygen; waveform capnography; then 1 breath every 6 seconds with continuous compressions. Airway oedema, large breasts and a full stomach make intubation harder, and a low FRC with high oxygen consumption makes her desaturate fast, so the airway is managed early by the best operator with a smaller tube. Once the airway is in, ventilation is 10 a minute without pausing compressions, as in any arrest.
    • d If on magnesium: stop it and give calcium. A woman on magnesium for pre-eclampsia who becomes bradycardic and arrests has magnesium toxicity until proved otherwise, particularly with oliguria, since magnesium is cleared by the kidney. Toxicity runs in order: loss of the patellar reflex, respiratory depression, conduction block, arrest. Calcium antagonises it at the membrane: calcium chloride 10% 10 mL or calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL IV.
    • e Perimortem caesarean: if no return of circulation, aim to deliver by 5 minutes. Prepare from the moment the arrest is recognised, and if circulation has not returned after about 4 minutes of resuscitation, deliver so that the uterus is empty by 5 minutes. Its purpose is maternal: emptying the uterus removes aortocaval compression and returns blood to her circulation, and survival of both mother and baby falls with each minute of delay. It is done where she lies, without moving to theatre, without checking for a fetal heart, and whether or not the fetus is viable, in any woman whose fundus is at or above the umbilicus. The current name is resuscitative hysterotomy.
    • f Detach fetal monitors; call the neonatal team. Fetal monitoring does not alter maternal resuscitation and gets in the way of defibrillation and delivery. The fetus is treated by resuscitating the mother.
    • g Compressions, defibrillation energy and drug doses are unchanged. Hand position, rate and depth are standard. Shocks are given at the usual energies: the fetus receives negligible current and maternal circulation is its only chance. Adrenaline 1 mg every 3–5 minutes is the vasopressor, as in any arrest, and no drug is withheld for fear of harming the fetus.

    OBS02B · 6 Hyperstimulation and reading the stem
    Ovarian hyperstimulation is a capillary leak graded by ascites and haematocrit, and in obstetric stems one planted phrase such as ‘no asthma’ or ‘ultrasound negative’ usually decides the answer.
    1. OHSS: gonadotropin/hCG drives a capillary leak → ascites, haemoconcentration, oliguria and clots.
    2. Grade by ascites and haematocrit: moderate = ascites on scan; severe = clinical ascites, Hct >0.45, oliguria; critical = tense ascites, Hct >0.55, anuria, thrombosis, ARDS.
