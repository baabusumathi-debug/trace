A heart muscle cell contracts when calcium meets its filaments. So there are only two ways to make it contract harder. Give the filaments more calcium, or make them more sensitive to the calcium they have. Three of these drugs take the first road, through one messenger, cyclic AMP. The fourth takes the second.

Dobutamine. It stimulates the beta-one receptor, cyclic AMP rises, and with it the contractility and the heart rate. It also stimulates beta-two receptors on the arterioles, which dilate. More pump, less squeeze: the card calls it inodilation. Two things follow, and both are asked. The tachycardia isn't a reflex. It's the direct beta-one effect, it climbs with the dose, and it's the toxic effect the banks key. And because dobutamine dilates, the pressure can fall on it.

Epinephrine. Beta-one again, and at higher doses alpha-one, so the vessels constrict. It also raises the lactate, through beta-two receptors driving glycolysis. That lactate isn't hypoperfusion, so it can mislead you. In infarct shock, the small OptimaCC trial found more refractory shock with epinephrine than with norepinephrine.

Milrinone works one step further in. Cyclic AMP is broken down by phosphodiesterase three. Block that enzyme, and cyclic AMP builds up with no receptor touched. In the heart, more contraction. In the vessels, dilation, most of all in the pulmonary vessels. Its main unwanted effect is hypotension, which is vasodilation and not toxicity. And the kidney clears it, so in renal failure you lower the rate and leave out the loading dose.

Levosimendan takes the other road. It binds troponin C and makes it more sensitive to calcium. No extra calcium enters the cell, so in theory it costs less oxygen and provokes fewer arrhythmias. It also opens potassium channels in the vessel wall, so the afterload falls. And an active metabolite keeps it working for days.

The ladder of treatment: offload first, then push

What this section settles
  1. Offload first, then push: lowering preload and afterload raises output at no oxygen cost; an inotrope raises it at a price.
  2. ESC profiles: wet + warm → diuretic and vasodilator. Wet + cold: SBP <90 perfusion first, diuretic later; SBP ≥90 vasodilator + diuretic. Dry + cold → fluid.

One fact, three complications. The blood from peripheral VA-ECMO runs backwards up the aorta. Follow it.

Start at the top of the stream. Oxygenated ECMO blood travels up from the groin. The heart's own output comes down from the aortic root. The two meet somewhere in the aorta. While the heart barely ejects, the meeting point lies near the heart, and the whole body gets ECMO blood.

Now the heart recovers, but the lungs are still failing. The ventricle ejects more, and what it ejects hasn't been oxygenated. The meeting point is pushed down the aorta. So the coronaries, the brain and the right arm get dark blood from the heart, while the lower body gets bright blood from the circuit. That's differential hypoxaemia, the Harlequin or north-south syndrome.

Where do you look? The right hand, because its artery comes from the first branch of the arch. The stem gives a saturation of eighty-four percent on the right hand and ninety-nine on the left foot.

The fix? Three tempting options, all wrong. Raise the oxygen on the circuit: that blood is already saturated. Raise the pump flow: that pushes harder against the ventricle. Convert to venovenous: the patient still needs the flow. The answer is to add a return cannula in the internal jugular vein. Oxygenated blood then enters the right heart, crosses the failing lungs, and is ejected already oxygenated. That's VAV.

Harlequin syndrome: the right hand is hypoxaemic while the foot is well oxygenated. Monitor the right hand. The fix is a venous return cannula, converting to VAV.

Second, stay at the heart. That backwards stream is afterload. A ventricle that can't eject against it still fills, so it distends. The aortic valve stops opening, the arterial trace goes flat, the pulmonary pressures rise, and the lungs flood. On the long axis view you'd see a swollen, still ventricle behind a closed valve. Vent it, with a balloon pump, an Impella or an atrial septostomy. An Impella added to ECMO is called ECPELLA.

Third, the groin. The arterial cannula fills most of the femoral artery, so the leg beyond it starves. Prevent it with a distal perfusion cannula. And if the foot turns cold and pulseless, the answer is an antegrade perfusion cannula at once, not heparin, elevation or thrombectomy.
