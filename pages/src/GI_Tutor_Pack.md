  - Before you start: The pancreas makes its digestive enzymes as inactive precursors (zymogens) and keeps a trypsin inhibitor inside the acinar cell, because trypsin is the enzyme that switches all the others on; acute pancreatitis is what happens when that protection fails. The disease runs in two phases: in the first week the harm comes from a sterile systemic inflammatory response and organ failure, and after that from local complications such as necrosis and its infection. Severity is therefore defined by organ failure and its duration, measured with the modified Marshall score (respiratory, cardiovascular and renal systems, a score of 2 or more is failure). Figures here follow the revised Atlanta classification (2012) and the ACG 2024 guideline, which the exam keys use.
- **The gut in critical illness**: tools: ✕✓ Disease map · ▶ Pattern drill (8); concept tiles: Concept 1 · 2 cards The gut barrier · Concept 2 · 7 cards The sick abdomen: imaging and perforation · Concept 3 · 2 cards Gastroparesis, paralysis and Ogilvie’s · Concept 4 · 2 cards Diarrhoea and AGI grading
  - Before you start: The gut is rarely the reason a patient is admitted to the ICU, but it is affected in most of them, and unlike the kidney or lung it has no single number that tells you it is failing. Its blood flow is the first to be cut when cardiac output falls, because splanchnic vasoconstriction protects the brain and heart, and it is often the last to recover. Feeding, motility and the mucosal barrier are linked: a gut that is not fed loses its villi, and a gut that cannot move cannot be fed. Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) is measured through the bladder; 12 mmHg or more is intra-abdominal hypertension (GI05).
- **Mesenteric ischaemia**: tools: ✕✓ Disease map · ▶ Pattern drill (7); concept tiles: Concept 1 · 2 cards Anatomy and the four causes · Concept 2 · 3 cards Diagnosis and treatment
  - Before you start: Mesenteric ischaemia is uncommon (about 0.1–0.2% of emergency admissions) but kills most of those in whom it is missed, because the window between reversible mucosal ischaemia and transmural infarction is measured in hours. The early patient has severe pain and a soft abdomen; by the time there is guarding, lactate and gas in the bowel wall, the bowel is dead. The ICU sees it in two ways: as the cause of an acute abdomen, and as a complication of shock and vasopressors (non-occlusive disease) in a sedated patient who cannot describe pain. This deck runs from anatomy to the operating table.
- **Abdominal pressure**: tools: ✕✓ Disease map · ▶ Pattern drill (7); concept tiles: Concept 1 · 4 cards Measure and define · Concept 2 · 3 cards Causes, effects and management
  - Before you start: The abdomen is a closed box whose walls stretch only so far: once its compliance is used up, a small rise in volume produces a large rise in pressure. That pressure is passed on to everything inside (kidneys, gut, liver, the inferior vena cava) and, through the diaphragm, to the chest. Perfusion of an abdominal organ depends on the gap between arterial pressure and the pressure around it, just as cerebral perfusion depends on MAP minus ICP. All figures in this deck are in mmHg; 1 mmHg is about 1.36 cmH2O.

## [PAN1] Acute pancreatitis Cause and diagnosis

*Summary:* Every cause ends in trypsin switched on inside the acinar cell; two of three criteria make the diagnosis, and lipase is the enzyme to trust.

### The concept, in order
1. **Gallstones (40–70%)** then **alcohol (25–35%)** cause most attacks. With neither, check **triglycerides (causal above 1,000 mg/dL)**, **calcium**, drugs and a recent **ERCP**.
2. Day-1 liver tests hint at the cause: **ALT above 150** points to stones, **AST above ALT** to alcohol. So the first scan is an **ultrasound for gallstones**.
3. Every route (duct obstruction, acinar injury, faulty enzyme transport) ends in **trypsin activated inside the acinar cell**, and trypsin switches the other enzymes on.
4. **Lipase** causes **fat necrosis**, which saponifies calcium, so **calcium falls**; **elastase** digests vessel walls and causes **bleeding**.
5. Diagnosis is **two of three**: typical **pain**, **lipase or amylase above 3× normal**, typical **CT or MRI**. With pain and enzymes, **no imaging is needed**.
6. **Lipase is the better test**: pancreas-specific, up in 4–8 h and raised for **8–14 days**. Amylase comes from many tissues and normalises in **3–4 days**, so a late presenter can have a normal amylase.
7. **The enzyme level does not grade severity.** 80–85% of attacks are mild (mortality under 1–3%); the 20% that are moderate or severe carry 13–35%.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **A raised amylase is not specific:** a perforated ulcer or mesenteric ischaemia can raise it. When the picture does not fit, CT decides.

**Visual: Three routes, one event, three kinds of damage**
- 1. **Duct obstruction** (gallstone, ampulla, chronic alcohol): oedema, impaired flow, ischaemia
- 2. **Acinar cell injury** (alcohol, drugs, trauma, virus): pro-enzymes and hydrolases released
- 3. **Faulty transport** (metabolic, alcohol, obstruction): pro-enzymes meet lysosomes
- 4. **Trypsin switched on inside the cell** (the common event): trypsin activates the rest
- 5. **Damage** (what the enzymes do): lipase: fat necrosis, low calcium; elastase: bleeding; phospholipase A₂: surfactant loss (ARDS)
Note: The first three are routes; the fourth is where they all meet. The histological hallmark is acinar cell destruction.

**Visual: Amylase and lipase over time**
- 2–12 h: **Amylase rises**: Amylase up within hours; peaks at 12–72 h (why: from salivary gland, bowel, ovary too: low specificity)
- 4–8 h: **Lipase rises**: Lipase up; peaks at about 24 h (why: pancreas-specific)
- 3–4 days: **Amylase back to normal**: A late presenter can have a normal amylase (why: urine amylase stays up for up to 2 weeks)
- 8–14 days: **Lipase back to normal**: Lipase is still the test in a late presenter (why: neither level grades severity)

### Gate questions (yellow box)
1. Typical pain and lipase 4× normal. Is CT needed to diagnose? → No. Two of three criteria are met; CT is for doubt, or after 72 h for complications.
2. Day 5, amylase normal, lipase high. Pancreatitis? → Yes. Amylase normalises in 3–4 days; lipase stays up for 8–14.
3. No stones, no alcohol. First blood test? → Serum triglycerides (causal above 1,000 mg/dL), then calcium.

  **c** Normalises: amylase 3–4 days; lipase 8–14 days.
  **d** Specificity: amylase low (salivary gland, bowel, ovary, fat, muscle); lipase high.

Why, part by part:
  - a **Rises: amylase 2–12 hours; lipase 4–8 hours.** Both are up by the time most patients arrive, so timing of the rise rarely decides anything.
  - b **Peaks: amylase 12–72 hours; lipase 24 hours.**
  - c **Normalises: amylase 3–4 days; lipase 8–14 days.** Amylase is a small molecule cleared by the kidney with a half-life of about 10–12 hours, so it can be back to normal by the time a late presenter arrives; lipase is reabsorbed by the renal tubule and persists. This is why lipase is the test for someone who has had pain for several days.
  - d **Specificity: amylase low (salivary gland, bowel, ovary, fat, muscle); lipase high.** Amylase is made in many tissues; its specificity is only about 20–60%, while lipase reaches 80–100% at the diagnostic threshold. ACG 2024 prefers lipase and says amylase alone is unreliable; ordering both adds nothing over lipase.
  - e **False positives: amylase in renal disease, appendicitis, cholecystitis; lipase fewer, not none — renal failure (both enzymes), bowel obstruction or ischaemia, perforated ulcer, cholecystitis, DKA.** Bowel ischaemia and perforation leak enzymes from the gut lumen into the peritoneum; renal failure slows clearance. These rises are usually below three times normal, which is why the diagnostic threshold is set at three. In macroamylasaemia, amylase bound to immunoglobulin cannot be filtered: serum amylase is high and urine amylase low, with no pancreatitis.

The size of the rise reflects how much enzyme leaked, not how much inflammation or necrosis there is, so a lipase of 10 times normal is not worse than 4 times. Severity comes from organ failure (PANC-05) and the scores (PANC-08 to PANC-11).

## [PAN2] Acute pancreatitis Grading and predicting severity

*Summary:* Severity is defined by how long organs fail; the scores only predict it.

### The concept, in order
1. **Revised Atlanta (2012)** grades by organ failure. **Mild**: none, and no complications. **Moderately severe**: organ failure that resolves **within 48 h**, and/or local or systemic complications. **Severe**: organ failure **beyond 48 h**.
2. Organ failure means a **modified Marshall score of 2 or more** in the **respiratory, cardiovascular or renal** system: three systems only.
3. The **determinant-based** system adds infection and has **four grades**; **critical** = persistent organ failure **and** infected necrosis. Infected necrosis alone is already **severe** there, but only moderately severe in Atlanta.
4. Scores predict; they do not define. **Ranson** has 11 items, **5 at admission and 6 at 48 h**, so it is complete only at **48 h**. 3 or more predicts severe disease, 6 or more about 40% mortality, and it contains no amylase or lipase.
5. **BISAP** is the bedside score in the **first 24 h**: **BUN above 25, impaired mental state, SIRS, age above 60, pleural effusion**. **3 or more** marks a sharp rise in deaths.
6. Single markers: **APACHE II of 8 or more**; **CRP above 150 mg/L at 48 h** predicts necrosis; **haematocrit above 44%** or a **rising BUN** on day 1 shows third-space loss.
7. The **CT severity index** adds the Balthazar grade (0–4) to necrosis (**0, 2, 4 or 6**), for a maximum of **10**. Necrosis weighs more, and **7–10** carries about **17%** mortality.
8. **Cullen and Grey Turner** signs (retroperitoneal blood) mean haemorrhagic necrotising disease but appear in **under 5%**: useful when present, useless when absent.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **BISAP cut-off:** the notes give 2; most sources and the exam use **3 or more**.
- **Age:** above 55 in Ranson, above 60 in BISAP. A favourite swap.

**Visual: Revised Atlanta: grade by how long organs fail**
- **> 48 h**: Severe persistent organ failure, single or multiple
- **< 48 h**: Moderately severe transient organ failure and/or local or systemic complications
- **None**: Mild no organ failure, no complications
Note: Organ failure = modified Marshall 2 or more in the respiratory, cardiovascular or renal system. The determinant-based system adds a fourth grade, critical: persistent organ failure and infected necrosis.

**Visual: The predicting numbers**
- **Ranson ≥ 3**: 11 items: 5 at admission, 6 at 48 h; ≥ 6 ≈ 40% mortality
- **BISAP ≥ 3**: 5 items in the first 24 h: BUN, mental state, SIRS, age > 60, effusion
- **APACHE II ≥ 8**: severe disease, any time
- **CRP > 150 at 48 h**: predicts necrosis (80% sensitive and specific)
- **Haematocrit > 44%**: or rising BUN: third-space loss on day 1
- **CT index 7–10**: Balthazar 0–4 + necrosis 0/2/4/6; ≈ 17% mortality
Note: These predict severity; only the course of organ failure defines it.

### Gate questions (yellow box)
1. Organ failure for 36 h, no collections. Grade? → Moderately severe: the failure was transient (under 48 h).
2. Which score cannot be completed before 48 hours? → Ranson: six of its 11 items are taken at 48 h.
3. Infected necrosis, no organ failure: Atlanta grade vs determinant-based grade? → Moderately severe (Atlanta) vs severe (determinant-based).

## [PAN3] Acute pancreatitis Imaging and collections

*Summary:* Ultrasound on day 1 for the cause, contrast CT after 72 hours for necrosis; collections are named by necrosis and by the 4-week wall.

### The concept, in order
1. **Ultrasound** on admission (within 48 h) looks for the **cause**: gallstones and a dilated duct.
2. **Contrast CT** is the gold standard for necrosis and collections, but only **after 72 h**: necrosis takes that long to demarcate, so an early CT under-calls it. An early CT is for diagnostic doubt only.
3. Repeat CT at **7–10 days** if the CT severity index is 3 or more, on deterioration, or before any intervention.
4. **MRI** avoids radiation and iodinated contrast (pregnancy, AKI) and shows **duct disruption**, but is **poor at showing gas**. **EUS** finds small duct stones that CT and ultrasound miss.
5. Collections are named by two questions: **necrosis or not**, and **before or after 4 weeks**. Fluid: **APFC → pseudocyst**. Necrosis: **acute necrotic collection → walled-off necrosis**.
6. That **4-week wall** is why drainage waits. **ERCP is a treatment, not a test**: only for **cholangitis** (within 24 h) or **persisting biliary obstruction**.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **Routine ERCP** in gallstone pancreatitis is the trap; the indication is cholangitis or a blocked duct.

**Visual: Name the collection: necrosis × time**
| Type ↓ Time → | Under 4 weeks | Over 4 weeks |
|---|---|---|
| No necrosis | Acute peripancreatic fluid collection no wall; usually resolves | Pseudocyst walled, fluid only |
| Necrosis | Acute necrotic collection necrosis, no wall | Walled-off necrosis walled, solid debris: drain, then debride if needed |
Note: The wall forms at about 4 weeks: that is why drainage and debridement wait.

**Visual: Which scan, when**
- Day 1 (≤ 48 h): **Ultrasound**: Gallstones, dilated duct: the cause (why: cheap, bedside, no contrast)
- After 72 h: **Contrast CT**: Necrosis, collections, CT severity index (why: earlier, necrosis has not demarcated)
- 7–10 days: **Repeat CT**: If CT index ≥ 3, on deterioration, before intervention
- Any time: **MRI or EUS**: Duct disruption, small duct stones; MRI in pregnancy or AKI (why: MRI is poor at gas)
- Only if indicated: **ERCP**: Cholangitis (within 24 h) or persisting obstruction (why: a treatment, not a test)

### Gate questions (yellow box)
1. Day 2, severe pancreatitis, CT shows no necrosis. Reassured? → No. Necrosis demarcates after 72 h; an early CT under-calls it.
2. Encapsulated fluid-only collection 6 weeks after oedematous pancreatitis? → A pseudocyst.
3. Gallstone pancreatitis without cholangitis: ERCP? → No. ERCP only for cholangitis or persisting obstruction.

## [PAN4] Acute pancreatitis Fluids, pain and feeding

*Summary:* Moderate Ringer’s to targets, generous analgesia, and an early-fed gut: a fed gut keeps bacteria out of the necrosis.

### The concept, in order
1. **Ringer’s lactate** is the fluid: fewer ICU admissions, less organ failure and a lower CRP than saline. Avoid it only in hypercalcaemic pancreatitis.
2. Inflamed capillaries leak whatever you give, so **moderate, goal-directed** fluid wins. **WATERFALL**: aggressive fluid (20 mL/kg, then 3 mL/kg/h) tripled **fluid overload (20.5% vs 6.3%)** with no better outcome.
3. Moderate means a **10 mL/kg bolus only if hypovolaemic**, then **1.5 mL/kg/h**, reassessed at 12, 24, 48 and 72 h.
4. Hypovolaemia markers: **haematocrit above 44%, BUN above 20, creatinine above 1.1, urine under 0.75 mL/kg/h, SBP under 90**. Slow or stop at **two signs of overload**.
5. Pain: **multimodal**, patient-controlled; **no NSAIDs in AKI**. An **epidural** is opioid-sparing and linked to less AKI and organ failure.
6. Mild disease: **on-demand oral feeding**, not nil by mouth. No oral intake by **day 3**: **nasogastric** feeding (as good as nasojejunal; nasojejunal for gastroparesis). **Parenteral nutrition is avoided.**

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **Septic-shock habit:** 30 mL/kg is the sepsis rule, not the pancreatitis rule. WATERFALL gives 10 mL/kg, and only to the hypovolaemic.
- **Fluid overload in WATERFALL** = at least two of the three criteria; the notes’ ‘one = suspected’ is not a protocol category (decided 22 Nov).

## [PAN5] Acute pancreatitis Infection, lung and the tight abdomen

*Summary:* Early fever is inflammation, not infection; infected necrosis comes late and gets a carbapenem. The lung fails first, and a tense abdomen changes how you ventilate.

### The concept, in order
1. Deaths come in two waves: **weeks 1–2 from SIRS and organ failure**, **after 2 weeks from infected necrosis**.
2. **No prophylactic antibiotics**, even in severe necrotising disease: they prevent neither infected necrosis nor death.
3. Suspect infected necrosis with **late deterioration** (weeks 2–4) or **gas in a collection on CT**. **Procalcitonin** helps hold antibiotics early; routine needle aspiration is no longer advised.
4. Then a **carbapenem**: it penetrates necrosis and covers ESBL Gram-negatives. **Antifungals are not routine.**
5. The **lung** fails most often: SIRS, fluid and **phospholipase A₂ digesting surfactant (DPPC)** cause ARDS, often with a **left pleural effusion**. Try non-invasive support, then **lung-protective** ventilation.
6. A **tense abdomen** passes **20–60%** of its pressure to the chest, so the chest wall, not the lung, is stiff. Keep **6–8 mL/kg** and **driving pressure under 15**, set PEEP by compliance (often higher), and allow a plateau target of **target − 7 + 0.7 × IAP**. An **oesophageal balloon** settles it.
7. **Pancreatic encephalopathy** (confusion and myoclonus in the first 2 weeks) is a diagnosis of exclusion: rule out **Wernicke’s** (give thiamine), glucose, sodium and alcohol withdrawal.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **Two plateau corrections:** this one raises the plateau **target** (− 7 + 0.7 × IAP); the abdominal-pressure chapter corrects the **measured** plateau (plateau − 0.5 × IAP). Don’t mix them.

## [PAN6] Acute pancreatitis Necrosis: delay, drain, step up

*Summary:* Wait for the wall, drain first, debride by the least invasive route, and open last.

### The concept, in order
1. **Sterile necrosis** is treated without intervention, and any intervention is **delayed to weeks 3–4** so the collection walls off.
2. **Step-up**: (1) **percutaneous or endoscopic drainage** for infected necrosis with deterioration; (2) **minimally invasive necrosectomy** (VARD or endoscopic transgastric); (3) **open necrosectomy last**.
3. **PANTER** (step-up vs open): major complications **69% → 40%**, fewer hernias and less new diabetes, the same mortality.
4. A **sterile** collection is touched only for a reason. After **4 weeks**: persistent organ failure, **obstruction** (gastric outlet, bile duct, bowel), a **disconnected duct**, or a symptomatic or growing **pseudocyst**. After **8 weeks**: ongoing pain.
5. Surgery is for failure of everything else: **abdominal compartment syndrome**, **bleeding when embolisation fails**, bowel ischaemia or fistula.
6. A **bleeding pseudoaneurysm** goes to **CT angiography and embolisation first**, surgery second.
7. **Mild gallstone pancreatitis**: cholecystectomy in the **same admission**. **Severe**: **defer** it until collections settle.
8. Blood purification (high-volume haemofiltration, CytoSorb) improves haemodynamics or short-term numbers, but **not long-term mortality**.

- **Delay is the rule**, but infected necrosis with deterioration can be drained earlier (step 1); debridement still waits for the wall.

## [GUT1] The gut in critical illness The gut barrier

*Summary:* In the ICU the gut matters as a barrier: low flow kills the villus tip first, the barrier leaks, and toxic lymph reaches the lung.

### The concept, in order
1. The gut absorbs, but in the ICU its **barrier** (mucosa, mucin) and **immune** roles (gut lymphoid tissue, secretory IgA) matter more.
2. Each segment has its loss: **duodenum** iron and calcium; **jejunum** most nutrients; **terminal ileum B12 and bile salts** (resection → B12 deficiency and bile-salt diarrhoea); **colon** water, sodium, short-chain fatty acids.
3. Splanchnic flow is cut first when output falls, and the **villus tip** dies first: the central arteriole fountains out at the tip, and **counter-current exchange** shunts oxygen to the venule before it arrives.
4. So **mucosal ischaemia comes before transmural ischaemia**, and **lactate rises late**.
5. The sequence: **hypoperfusion → resuscitation → ischaemia–reperfusion and oedema → barrier loss**. Bacteria and endotoxin then **translocate** into portal blood and lymph.
6. **Toxic lymph** travels up the thoracic duct and reaches the **lung first**: the gut is the motor of multiple organ failure.
7. The defence is **early enteral feeding**: it keeps the villi intact and cuts translocation.
8. The diaphragm clears peritoneal fluid and bacteria through **stomata** into lymph on expiration, so peritoneal infection reaches the blood fast. **IAH and ventilation** splint it and slow clearance.

**Visual: From low flow to distant organ failure**
- 1. **Low flow** (splanchnic flow cut first): shock, vasopressors
- 2. **Villus tip** (counter-current shunt): dies first; lactate still normal
- 3. **Barrier fails** (ischaemia–reperfusion, oedema): tight junctions open
- 4. **Translocation** (portal blood and lymph): bacteria, endotoxin
- 5. **Toxic lymph** (via the thoracic duct): lung first → multiple organ failure
Note: The defence: early enteral feeding keeps the villi and the barrier.

### Gate questions (yellow box)

2. Toxic gut lymph reaches which organ first? → The lung, via the thoracic duct.
3. Single best defence of the gut barrier? → Early enteral feeding.

## [GUT2] The gut in critical illness The sick abdomen: imaging and perforation

*Summary:* The ICU abdomen lies on examination, so numbers and imaging decide; the perforation site sets the contamination.

### The concept, in order
1. Most ICU patients have a GI symptom; **three or more at once** is gut failure and predicts lower survival.
2. The bedside abdomen is unreliable under sedation and ventilation, so **imaging decides**: ultrasound for fluid and organs, X-ray for tubes and loops.
3. The **3-6-9 rule**: small bowel above **3 cm**, colon above **6 cm**, caecum above **9 cm** is dilated; a **caecum above 12 cm** risks perforation.
4. **Contrast CT** whenever sepsis may be abdominal: free air or fluid, thick wall, **pneumatosis**, reduced mesenteric flow.
5. Visceral pain follows embryology: **foregut → epigastrium, midgut → periumbilical, hindgut → hypogastrium**; parietal pain localises.
6. Bacteria increase down the gut (stomach under 10³/mL, colon **10¹¹–10¹²**, mostly anaerobes), so a **colonic perforation** gives the worst faecal peritonitis.
7. **Perforated ulcer**: sudden epigastric pain, shoulder-tip pain, board-like abdomen; an **erect chest X-ray** shows free gas in about 75–85% (CT if negative).
8. Named pictures: a contained small **oesophageal perforation** in a stable patient is treated **without surgery** (stent or clip, nil by mouth, antibiotics and an antifungal). Crepitus, gas in tissues and shock mean **Clostridium perfringens** (debride; penicillin plus clindamycin). Bleeding 2 weeks after a Whipple means a **pseudoaneurysm**: CT angiography and embolisation.

- **Caecum > 12 cm**: perforation risk: decompress

  - a **Ultrasound: free fluid, perfusion, organ structure; bedside.** It needs no transport and finds free fluid, gallbladder disease, hydronephrosis and the bladder, but gas and dressings defeat it in the post-operative abdomen. It is a screening tool, not the test that excludes a collection.
  - b **Abdominal X-ray: tube position; dilated loops.** Its main ICU use is to confirm where a feeding tube lies: a nasogastric tube is checked by X-ray or an aspirate pH of 5.5 or less before any feed.   - c **Dilated bowel, the 3-6-9 rule: small bowel over 3 cm; colon over 6 cm; caecum over 9 cm.** These are the ESICM definitions of dilatation. The caecum has the widest diameter and so, by Laplace’s law, the highest wall tension for any pressure, which is why it is the part that perforates.

**Card Top-up · Clostridial gas gangrene of the abdomen DocT 1 questions** (TOP-UP)
Before the card: Step 3 includes the abdomen that is septic and full of gas. Gas in the bowel wall, mesentery and abdominal wall with crepitus and shock in a diabetic points to a gas-forming organism that destroys tissue. The one DocT QBank question asks which organism (77% right).
Question: Diabetic with peritonitis, shock, abdominal-wall crepitus and gas in bowel wall, mesentery and soft tissue: organism?
Answer (behind “Show answer”):
  - Clostridium perfringens: anaerobic, spore-forming Gram-positive rod
  - Alpha-toxin (lecithinase) → myonecrosis, haemolysis and shock
  - Immediate radical debridement + penicillin with clindamycin (stops toxin synthesis) ± hyperbaric O2
  - Gas without myonecrosis in diabetics (emphysematous pyelonephritis/cholecystitis) is usually E. coli/Klebsiella
Why, part by part:
  - a **Clostridium perfringens: anaerobic, spore-forming Gram-positive rod.** It grows in dead, poorly perfused tissue and ferments it, producing gas that tracks along tissue planes and gives crepitus.
  - b **Alpha-toxin (lecithinase) causes myonecrosis, haemolysis and shock.** Alpha-toxin is a phospholipase C that breaks down cell membranes, so muscle dies and red cells lyse; it also depresses the myocardium and causes profound shock.
  - c **Immediate radical debridement plus penicillin with clindamycin (stops toxin synthesis), with or without hyperbaric oxygen.** Antibiotics cannot reach dead tissue, so the dead tissue must go. Clindamycin inhibits protein synthesis and so stops toxin production even when bacteria are not dividing. Hyperbaric oxygen inhibits alpha-toxin production by this anaerobe, but must not delay surgery.
  - d **Gas without myonecrosis in diabetics (emphysematous pyelonephritis or cholecystitis) is usually E. coli or Klebsiella.** Gram-negative organisms ferment the high glucose in diabetic tissue and make gas, but they do not destroy muscle the way clostridia do.
Traps on this card (1):
  - “E. coli or Klebsiella” for gas-forming peritonitis with shock and crepitus → They make gas in the kidney or gallbladder without myonecrosis; tissue destruction with shock is C. perfringens.
Punch: Gas, crepitus, myonecrosis and shock mean C. perfringens: alpha-toxin kills tissue faster than antibiotics can reach it, so debride at once and give penicillin with clindamycin.

## [GUT3] The gut in critical illness Gastroparesis, paralysis and Ogilvie’s

*Summary:* Most ICU dysmotility is made by what we give or fail to correct. Remove it, give a short prokinetic course, then a tube past the pylorus; Ogilvie’s gets neostigmine.

### The concept, in order
1. ICU gastroparesis is mostly **transient and made by us**: **opioids**, β- and α₂-agonists, **high glucose**, low potassium or magnesium, acidosis. The lasting causes are diabetes, renal failure, vagotomy and scleroderma.
2. It shows as **high gastric residuals**: a single residual above **200 mL** is high; **hold gastric feed above 500 mL**.
3. Treat in order: **correct glucose and electrolytes, cut opioids and sedatives** → **erythromycin** (motilin agonist) ± **metoclopramide** (D₂ antagonist) → a **post-pyloric tube** (residual above 500 mL or failed prokinetics).
4. Prokinetics are a **short course**: tachyphylaxis (erythromycin fades by 72 h; ESPEN 2023 says 24–48 h, stop by day 3), and erythromycin prolongs QT.
5. **Lower GI paralysis** = **no stool for 3 days** without mechanical obstruction; hyperperistalsis suggests obstruction.
6. **Ogilvie’s** = massive **colonic** dilatation without obstruction. Correct the causes, then give **neostigmine 2 mg IV** with ECG monitoring and atropine at hand; **colonoscopic decompression** if it fails or the **caecum is above 12 cm**.
7. Prevention: correct electrolytes and glucose, avoid fluid overload, **minimise opioids and sedatives**, early low-dose enteral feeding, laxatives.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **Prokinetic duration (card fixed 22 Nov):** the card said up to 5 days; ESPEN 2023 says 24–48 h, stop by day 3. Answer ‘short course, tachyphylaxis’.
- **Bowel sounds (card fixed):** ESICM 2012 says at least 1 minute in at least two quadrants, repeated; not every quadrant.

## [GUT4] The gut in critical illness Diarrhoea and AGI grading

*Summary:* Diarrhoea is defined by number, weight and form, and is not a reason to stop feeding; the AGI grade tells you to feed, treat, search or operate.

### The concept, in order
1. ICU diarrhoea = **3 or more loose stools a day**, **above 200 g/day**, **Bristol 5–7**.
2. First step: **stop laxatives and prokinetics, review drugs and feed, test for C. difficile**, and **keep feeding**. No antimotility agent until C. difficile is excluded.
3. **Fasting separates them**: **osmotic** diarrhoea stops with fasting (osmotic gap above 100); **secretory** diarrhoea is large, plasma-like and ignores fasting (gap under 50). Gap = 290 − 2 × (Na + K).
4. **AGI (ESICM 2012)**: **I risk** (transient), **II dysfunction** (needs intervention), **III failure** (no response to intervention), **IV failure with distant organ dysfunction** (life-threatening).
5. Abdominal pressure maps onto it: **AGI II ≈ IAH grade I (12–15)**, **AGI III ≈ IAH grade II (15–20)**, and **abdominal compartment syndrome is AGI IV**.
6. Act by grade: **I, feed**. **II, keep feeding, treat the symptom, measure IAP** (50–80% of needs early, 80–100% by day 3). **III, search for hidden abdominal pathology**; minimal feed, supplemental PN after day 4–7. **IV, stop enteral feed**, decompress, then endoscopy, IR or **laparotomy**.
7. Reassess **6-hourly**, then 12-hourly, and watch for **refeeding syndrome** when feeding restarts.

**Visual: AGI grade → action**
- **IV**: Failure + distant organs stop enteral feed; decompress; laparotomy, IR, endoscopy
- **III**: Failure search for hidden abdominal cause; minimal feed; PN after day 4–7
- **II**: Dysfunction keep feeding, treat the symptom, measure IAP
- **I**: Risk feed; fix fluids and electrolytes
Note: AGI II ≈ IAH grade I (12–15); AGI III ≈ IAH grade II (15–20); ACS is AGI IV.

## [MES1] Mesenteric ischaemia Anatomy and the four causes

*Summary:* Three arteries, two watersheds, four causes: each with its own patient and its own pain. Colonic ischaemia is a separate, milder disease.

### The concept, in order
1. **Coeliac**: stomach, liver, proximal duodenum. **SMA**: distal duodenum, **all the small bowel**, right and proximal transverse colon. **IMA**: distal transverse colon to the upper rectum.
2. The watersheds fail first in low flow: **Griffiths’ point = splenic flexure**, **Sudeck’s point = rectosigmoid**.
3. The SMA leaves the aorta at an acute angle, so **emboli lodge there**, usually **beyond the middle colic**, sparing the proximal jejunum; a thrombosis sits at the origin.
4. The gut tolerates a **50%** fall in flow (75% for up to 12 h) by extracting more oxygen; injury starts below a **MAP of 45**. The **mucosa dies first** while the serosa still looks pink.
5. Reperfusion adds a **xanthine oxidase free-radical** injury: expect **high potassium, acidosis and hypotension** when flow returns.
6. Four causes: **embolism (25–50%: AF, sudden pain)**; **thrombosis (15–40%: post-prandial pain, weight loss)**; **non-occlusive (20–25%: shock, high-dose vasopressors, cardiac surgery, dialysis)**, the ICU’s own and the deadliest; **venous (under 10%: thrombophilia, cirrhosis, pancreatitis; pain waxing and waning)**.
7. The sign of all four: **pain out of proportion** to a soft abdomen. **Peritonism means infarction.**
8. **Colonic ischaemia** is different: older (90% over 60), not very sick, **mild left-sided tenderness and bloody diarrhoea within 24 h**, and the test is **colonoscopy**. Aortic surgery causes ischaemic colitis, not NOMI.

**Visual: Four causes, four stories (swipe on a phone)**
| Cause | Share | Who | Pain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Embolism | 25–50% | AF, valve disease, endocarditis | Sudden, severe, with vomiting |
| Thrombosis | 15–40% | Atherosclerosis prior post-prandial pain, weight loss | Worsening, then as embolic |
| Non-occlusive | 20–25% | Shock, high-dose vasopressors, cardiac surgery, dialysis | Variable, masked by sedation |
| Venous | < 10% | Thrombophilia, cirrhosis, pancreatitis | Waxing and waning over days |
Note: All share pain out of proportion to the findings; peritonism means infarction.

## [MES2] Mesenteric ischaemia Diagnosis and treatment

*Summary:* No blood test rules it in or out: suspicion goes straight to biphasic CT angiography, peritonitis straight to theatre. Restore flow by the cause, resect only dead bowel, look again.

### The concept, in order
1. No blood test confirms or excludes AMI: **D-dimer** is sensitive (96%) but non-specific; **I-FABP** is specific (93%) but insensitive; **lactate is late**, a sign of transmural infarction.
2. **Biphasic CT angiography** is the test (sensitivity 94%, specificity 95%): **no oral contrast**, and **don’t withhold contrast for AKI**.
3. CT signs: arterial filling defect, non-enhancing or thick wall, dilatation; **pneumatosis with portal venous gas = dead bowel**.
4. **Peritonitis → laparotomy without imaging.** A normal CT with high suspicion → **catheter angiography** (especially for NOMI).
5. **Tonometry** reads the mucosa’s CO₂: a widening **gastric-to-arterial PCO₂ gap** (normal under 7 mmHg) means gut ischaemia. The card calls it the only valid test, but it is rarely used.
6. General care: fluids, NG decompression, broad-spectrum antibiotics, **weight-based unfractionated heparin**. **Avoid vasopressin, high-dose noradrenaline and digoxin**; if an inotrope is needed, use **dobutamine, low-dose dopamine or milrinone**.
7. **Peritonitis decides the route.** None → **endovascular** (aspiration or stent; mortality about 25% vs 40% open in observational data). Peritonitis → **open**: restore flow first, **resect only dead bowel**, leave the abdomen open, **second look**.
8. By cause: thrombosis → **stent or antegrade bypass** (vein, not PTFE, in a contaminated field); venous → **heparin**, then anticoagulation for **3–6 months** (lifelong with thrombophilia); **NOMI → restore flow, stop constrictors, papaverine into the SMA**.

## [IAH1] Abdominal pressure Measure and define

*Summary:* Abdominal pressure is a number from the bladder: 12 is hypertension, and over 20 with a new failing organ is compartment syndrome.

### The concept, in order
1. Normal IAP in the critically ill is **5–7 mmHg**; obesity and pregnancy raise the baseline **without** organ effects.
2. Measure through the **bladder** (the gold standard), **every 4 h** while at risk: **supine**, zero at the **mid-axillary line**, instil **at most 25 mL** of saline, wait **30–60 s**, read at **end-expiration** with a **relaxed abdominal wall**.
3. Every departure pushes the reading **up**: more saline, head up, abdominal muscles contracting.
4. **IAH = sustained or repeated IAP of 12 or more**: **grade I 12–15, II 16–20, III 21–25, IV above 25**.
5. **ACS = sustained IAP above 20 with new organ dysfunction.** It is not a grade: even grade IV without organ failure is only IAH.
6. **APP = MAP − IAP** (target **60 or more**). The kidney feels IAP twice, **filtration gradient = MAP − 2 × IAP**, so it fails first.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **Children:** 1 mL/kg (minimum 3, maximum 25 mL); the notes print 0.5 mL/kg.

- **21–25**: Grade III
- **16–20**: Grade II
- **12–15**: Grade I
Note: ACS = sustained IAP > 20 with NEW organ dysfunction. It is not a grade. Normal is 5–7.

## [IAH2] Abdominal pressure Causes, effects and management

*Summary:* Pressure rises when the abdomen holds more or gives less. It lowers output while CVP and airway pressures read high. Treat medically first; decompress refractory compartment syndrome.

### The concept, in order
1. **Primary** ACS starts **in the abdomen or pelvis** (trauma, damage-control laparotomy, packs): early surgery or radiology. **Secondary** comes from **massive resuscitation** elsewhere (burns, sepsis): medical first. **Recurrent** returns after treatment of either.
2. Four groups of cause: **less wall compliance** (burn eschar, tight closure), **more intraluminal contents** (ileus, gastric distension), **more abdominal contents** (ascites, blood, packs), **capillary leak with fluid resuscitation**. In trauma, **3 L or more of crystalloid** predicts both types.
3. Effects: **venous return and cardiac output fall**; the chest stiffens (high peak and plateau pressures); **oliguria at 15, anuria at 30 mmHg**; gut flow falls and oedema raises the pressure further; **ICP rises**.
4. The numbers that rise mislead: about half of IAP reaches the chest, so **CVP and wedge read falsely high**. Transmural CVP = **CVP − 0.5 × IAP**, or use volumetric indices.
5. Medical steps from **IAP 12**: **relax the wall** (sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular block; head of bed ≤ 30°), **empty the gut** (NG, rectal tube, prokinetics), **drain collections** (paracentesis, percutaneous), **stop the positive balance** (diuretics, ultrafiltration).
6. ACS: find the cause. A perforation or bleed is opened now; fluid-driven disease is drained and dried first; **decompressive laparotomy** with temporary closure if organ failure persists (usually grade III–IV).
7. Stop measuring when IAP stays **under 12**. Protocolised WSACS care raised survival **from 50% to 72%**. Expect **reperfusion** (hypotension, high potassium, acidosis) on decompression.

### Key vs guideline (red box)
- **No fixed pressure threshold** for laparotomy: persistent grade III–IV with organ dysfunction is the usual trigger.
- **Two plateau corrections:** measured plateau − 0.5 × IAP (this chapter) vs plateau target − 7 + 0.7 × IAP (pancreatitis chapter).

**Visual: What the pressure does**
- **Heart**: venous return and output fall; CVP and wedge read high
- **Lung**: stiff chest wall; high peak and plateau; hypoxia
- **Kidney**: oliguria at 15, anuria at 30; fails first
- **Gut**: flow falls; oedema raises the pressure further
- **Brain**: venous drainage impeded: ICP rises
- **Numbers**: transmural CVP = CVP − 0.5 × IAP

**Visual: WSACS: medical first, then decompress**

- Relax the wall: **Sedation, analgesia**: Neuromuscular block if needed; head of bed ≤ 30°

*More pairs from the cards (fold):*
- [IAH-05] **Recurrent (formerly tertiary) vs primary**: Recurrent returns after treatment of either type, even with an open abdomen.
- [IAH-06] **Primary vs secondary predictors**: Both share crystalloid of 3 L or more in the ED; primary adds markers of the lethal triad, secondary adds the ICU crystalloid total of 7.5 L.
- [IAH-07] **Intraluminal (gas, gastric content) vs abdominal (ascites, blood) contents**: Intraluminal is emptied by NG and rectal tubes; abdominal fluid by paracentesis or percutaneous drainage.
- [IAH-08] **High CVP in IAH vs high CVP in volume overload**: In IAH the transmural CVP is low and the ventricle underfilled; giving fluid on a high CVP is wrong in both, but for different reasons.
- [IAH-09] **Raised CVP from IAH vs from fluid overload**: In IAH the transmural pressure and the ventricular volume are low.
- [IAH-10] **Percutaneous drainage vs decompressive laparotomy**: Drainage when free fluid is the cause and organ failure is not immediately life-threatening; laparotomy for a surgical cause or failure of medical measures.
- [IAH-11] **Surgical-cause ACS vs medical-cause ACS**: Perforation or bleeding: laparotomy now. Pancreatitis or fluid overload: medical measures and percutaneous drainage first, laparotomy if refractory.
- [IAH-12] **After decompression: reperfusion syndrome vs bleeding**: Reperfusion syndrome is sudden hypotension, hyperkalaemia and acidosis as ischaemic tissue is reperfused and systemic resistance falls; fill and prepare vasopressors before the fascia is opened.

  **b** IAP over 20 with new organ failure: abdominal compartment syndrome: find the cause.
  **c** Primary compartment syndrome: decompressive laparotomy with temporary abdominal closure.

Watch: No exact pressure threshold for opening the abdomen is established: generally persistent grade III–IV with organ dysfunction.
Why, part by part:
  - a **IAP 12 or more: start medical treatment; measure IAP at least every 4 hours; avoid excess fluid.** The WSACS algorithm says at least every 4–6 hours or continuously, titrating therapy to keep IAP at 15 or less.
  - b **IAP over 20 with new organ failure: abdominal compartment syndrome: find the cause.** The cause decides the route: a surgical cause (perforation, bleeding, ischaemic bowel, packed abdomen) or a medical one (capillary leak, ascites, pancreatitis).
  - c **Primary compartment syndrome: decompressive laparotomy with temporary abdominal closure.** WSACS recommends decompressive laparotomy for overt ACS (1D). With a perforation, laparotomy also controls the source, so it is done at once, not after conservative measures fail. The abdomen is left open with a temporary closure, ideally negative-pressure therapy (1C), and the fascia is closed as early as physiology allows (1D).
  - d **Secondary or recurrent: continue medical options; decompress if organ failure progresses.** In pancreatitis or resuscitation-induced ACS, NG and rectal decompression, sedation and paralysis, percutaneous drainage and a negative balance come first; refractory ACS is decompressed. In a burn patient with ACS from intra-abdominal fluid, percutaneous drainage is a reasonable first step when the fluid is drainable, and decompressive laparotomy follows if organ failure persists; escharotomy only helps when a circumferential torso eschar is the restriction.
  - e **IAP consistently under 12: stop measuring, observe.** Below 12 the patient no longer has IAH by definition, so WSACS-based protocols stop routine measurement and observe, and measurement resumes if the patient deteriorates clinically or a new risk factor appears.
Top-up lines: DocT + DocT GT: After decompression, keep the abdomen open with temporary closure (negative-pressure preferred, WSACS 2013) and close the fascia as early as physiology allows: DocT keys 48–72 h, WSES 2018 says ideally within 4–7 days. **Card IAH-10 GI05 🔥 Marrow ×1 DocT ×3** 
Before the card: Step 5 begins with what is done before any surgeon is called. Four questions; the hardest (34% right) was the grade II patient with falling compliance in whom the right answer is neuromuscular blockade, not decompression.
Question: **Medical management: five options.**
Answer (behind “Show answer”):
  **a** Improve wall compliance: sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade; avoid head of bed over 30 degrees.
  **b** Evacuate intraluminal contents: nasogastric and rectal decompression, prokinetics.
  **c** Evacuate abdominal collections: paracentesis, percutaneous drainage.
  **d** Correct positive fluid balance: avoid excess fluid; diuretics, colloid, ultrafiltration.
  **e** Organ support: optimise ventilation; use transmural pressures.
Watch: Start at IAP of 12 or more. Enteral feed is minimised while pressure is high.
Why, part by part:
  - a **Improve wall compliance: sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade; avoid head of bed over 30 degrees.** Pain and agitation tighten the abdominal wall; WSACS suggests analgesia and sedation and a brief trial of neuromuscular blockade as a temporising measure (2D). Paralysis can lower IAP by several mmHg within minutes, which buys time in grade II–III IAH. Remove tight dressings; an abdominal eschar needs escharotomy.
  - b **Evacuate intraluminal contents: nasogastric and rectal decompression, prokinetics.** WSACS suggests liberal NG and rectal decompression when the stomach or colon is dilated, and neostigmine for refractory colonic ileus (2D). This is the first, least invasive step (the Marrow post-splenectomy question).
  - c **Evacuate abdominal collections: paracentesis, percutaneous drainage.** Percutaneous catheter drainage is suggested over doing nothing (2C) and even over immediate decompressive laparotomy (2D) when there is obvious free fluid, for example ascites in pancreatitis or burns. It is therapeutic, not just diagnostic, and is not contraindicated in ACS.
  - d **Correct positive fluid balance: avoid excess fluid; diuretics, colloid, ultrafiltration.** WSACS suggests a protocol to avoid a positive cumulative balance after resuscitation (2C) and a high plasma:red cell ratio in massive haemorrhage (2D). It makes no recommendation on diuretics, albumin or RRT, so furosemide is not the answer for an oliguric patient whose kidneys are compressed.
  - e **Organ support: optimise ventilation; use transmural pressures.** Set PEEP to counter the pressure on the lung bases, interpret CVP and plateau transmurally (IAH-09), and aim for APP of at least 60 (an observational target; WSACS 2013 makes no recommendation on APP).
Traps on this card (3):
  - “IAP 19 after laparotomy, poor compliance, falling urine: surgical decompression” → 19 is grade II, not ACS; a brief trial of neuromuscular blockade is the keyed answer (only 34% right). Furosemide does not treat compression, and antibiotics treat nothing here.
  - “Percutaneous drainage is only diagnostic” or “contraindicated in ACS” → It is a valid non-surgical decompression (77% right).
  - “Immediate laparotomy for septic free fluid with organ dysfunction” → If the fluid can be drained, ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage comes first (72% right).
Punch: Before anyone opens the abdomen, relax the wall, empty the gut, drain the fluid and stop the positive balance; each lowers IAP by removing a cause.

**Card IAH-08 GI05 🔥 Marrow ×1 DocT GT ×1** 
Before the card: Step 4 is what the pressure does. Three questions: the kidney mechanism (88% right), an ‘all except’ in which a third missed that airway pressures rise, and the Marrow question on the lung in a ventilated patient.
Question: **End-organ effects:**
Answer (behind “Show answer”):
  **a** Heart: venous return falls (caval compression); raised diaphragm impairs ventricular compliance; CVP and wedge pressure read falsely high.
  **b** Lung: low compliance and FRC, atelectasis: hypoxia, hypercapnia, high peak and plateau pressures.
  **c** Kidney: oliguria at 15 mmHg; anuria at 30 mmHg.
  **d** Gut: mesenteric and mucosal flow fall; venous stasis and oedema raise the pressure further; translocation.
  **e** Brain: raised intrathoracic pressure impedes venous drainage: ICP rises.
Watch: The gut effect is a vicious circle: oedema raises pressure, pressure causes more oedema.
Why, part by part:
  - a **Heart: venous return falls (caval compression); raised diaphragm impairs ventricular compliance; CVP and wedge pressure read falsely high.** Preload falls and afterload rises (compressed vascular beds raise systemic resistance), so cardiac output falls. The pressure transmitted to the chest raises CVP even though the ventricle is underfilled (IAH-09).
  - b **Lung: low compliance and FRC, atelectasis: hypoxia, hypercapnia, high peak and plateau pressures.** The diaphragm is pushed up, the chest wall becomes stiff, and the respiratory system compliance falls, so the same tidal volume needs higher pressure. Ventilation pressures rise, not fall.
  - c **Kidney: oliguria at 15 mmHg; anuria at 30 mmHg.** Renal vein compression, direct parenchymal compression and the filtration gradient (MAP − 2 × IAP, IAH-04) reduce filtration; low cardiac output and renin–angiotensin activation add to it.
  - d **Gut: mesenteric and mucosal flow fall; venous stasis and oedema raise the pressure further; translocation.** Gut perfusion falls early, at moderate pressures, often before urine output falls: mucosal acidosis and oedema add volume and raise IAP more, so the problem feeds itself, and bacterial translocation drives sepsis and multi-organ failure. The liver loses arterial and portal flow, so lactate clearance falls.
  - e **Brain: raised intrathoracic pressure impedes venous drainage: ICP rises.** Jugular venous outflow is impeded; decompressive laparotomy has lowered refractory intracranial hypertension in head-injured patients with IAH.
Traps on this card (2):
  - “Raised IAP decreases ventilation pressure” → Peak and plateau pressures rise; this is the exception in the GT7 ‘all except’ (66% right).
  - “Oliguria in ACS is from low contractility or ATN” → It is the pressure (renal vein and parenchymal compression, filtration gradient).
Punch: Raised abdominal pressure lowers cardiac output, urine and gut flow while it raises CVP, airway pressure and ICP; the numbers that rise are the ones that mislead.

## [IAH-AXIS] Abdominal pressure Abdominal pressure: where it rises and what it does

- **Abdominal contents**: fluid, blood, oedema, gas
  - ✕ More contents: ascites, ileus, packs, capillary leak
  - ✓/⊘ Drain, decompress the gut, stop the positive balance
- **Abdominal wall**: compliance
  - ✕ Burn eschar, tight closure, tense muscles
  - ✓/⊘ Sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular block; head of bed ≤ 30°
- **Organs inside**: APP = MAP − IAP
  - ✕ Kidney first (MAP − 2 × IAP): oliguria at 15, anuria at 30
  - ✕ Gut flow falls
  - ✓/⊘ Keep APP ≥ 60
- **Chest and brain**: about half transmitted
  - ✕ CVP, wedge and plateau read falsely high; ICP rises
  - ✓/⊘ Use transmural values (− 0.5 × IAP)

⟲ The abdomen is a closed box: once its compliance is used up, a little more volume gives a lot more pressure.
