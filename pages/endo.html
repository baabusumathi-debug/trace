Endocrine · NEET-SS, with tricks

Thyroid

Before you startAlmost all circulating thyroid hormone is bound to proteins (TBG, transthyretin, albumin); only the tiny free fraction acts, so anything that changes binding changes the effect without changing the gland. T4 is a prohormone made only by the thyroid, and deiodinases in liver, kidney and other tissues remove one iodine to make T3, the hormone that binds the nuclear receptor; several storm drugs work here as well as in the gland. Thyroid hormone sets the number of β-adrenergic receptors and the metabolic rate, which is why excess looks like a catecholamine surge and deficiency looks like everything slowing down. Doses and criteria follow the ATA 2016 hyperthyroidism and ATA 2014 hypothyroidism guidelines, with the Japan Thyroid Association 2016 guideline where it differs.

    Thyroid Reading thyroid labs
    TSH is the amplified signal: read it first, then free T4, and in the ICU suspect the illness before the gland.
    The concept, in order
    1. The gland makes mostly T4, a long-lived prohormone (half-life 7 days); tissues turn it into T3, the potent hormone (3–4×), so 75–80% of T3 is made outside the thyroid. Only the free fraction acts.
    2. TRH drives TSH, TSH drives the gland, and T4/T3 feed back on both. A small change in free T4 moves TSH a lot, so TSH is the first test.
    3. Primary disease moves TSH against T4: undetectable in overt hyperthyroidism, high in hypothyroidism. When the pituitary fails, TSH is low or inappropriately normal with a low T4, so a normal TSH cannot exclude central disease.
    4. Thyrotoxicosis has two engines. Overproduction (Graves’, toxic nodules) makes new hormone, so thionamides work. A leak from damaged follicles (thyroiditis, lithium, amiodarone type 2) releases stored hormone, so thionamides are useless and the β-blocker carries the patient.
    5. In the first trimester TSH falls (often < 0.1) because hCG stimulates the TSH receptor: physiological, peaking at 10–12 weeks.
    6. In the ICU the commonest abnormal result is non-thyroidal illness: cytokines cut type 1 deiodinase and raise type 3, so T3 falls first and reverse T3 rises; TRH falls, so TSH stays normal or low.
    7. Severity follows the illness: low T3 alone is mild; low T4 with a low TSH is severe (total T4 < 4 µg/dL, about 50% mortality). It marks prognosis; it is not a thyroid problem to treat.
    8. So don’t test routinely and don’t give thyroxine: treat and feed the patient. In recovery TSH can overshoot (usually < 20); repeat 4–6 weeks after recovery and treat only TSH > 20 with a low free T4.
    Key vs guideline
    • Undetectable vs low: in overt hyperthyroidism both are true, but when both are offered the exam wants undetectable (only 18% chose it).
    • Why TSH falls in illness: DocT keys cytokines acting directly on the pituitary over ‘decreased TRH’ when both appear; both happen.
    • Card fixed: Riedel thyroiditis is no longer listed as a cause of thyrotoxicosis; secondary hypothyroidism reads ‘TSH low or inappropriately normal’.

    Why, part by part
    • a Primary hyperthyroid: TSH low, T3 and T4 high. In overt disease TSH is not just low but undetectable (under 0.01–0.05 mIU/L on modern assays); a low but measurable TSH of 0.1–0.4 suggests subclinical disease, non-thyroidal illness, or dopamine or steroids. The exception is a TSH-secreting adenoma, where TSH is normal or high with high hormone.
    • b Primary hypothyroid: TSH high, T3 and T4 low. The failing gland loses feedback, so the pituitary drives TSH up.
    • c Secondary (pituitary) hypothyroid: TSH low, T3 and T4 low. TSH may also be ‘normal’, which is inappropriate for a low T4 (ENDO-13 h). This is why a normal TSH cannot exclude hypothyroidism in pituitary disease or myxoedema coma from pituitary failure (ENDO-11).
    • d Subclinical hyper- or hypothyroid: TSH low or high, hormone normal. The pituitary detects a change before free T4 leaves its reference range.
    • e Overproduction: Graves’, toxic multinodular goitre, TSH-secreting adenoma. These glands make new hormone, so thionamides work.
    • f Leak from destruction: subacute or lymphocytic thyroiditis, radiation. Stored hormone leaks from damaged follicles, so thionamides do not help and β-blockers carry the patient; the thyrotoxic phase is usually followed by hypothyroidism. Riedel thyroiditis is a fibrosing disease and does not usually cause thyrotoxicosis. Lithium causes painless thyroiditis in the same way, as do checkpoint inhibitors and interferon-α.
    • g Drugs: thyroxine, amiodarone, iodinated contrast; metastatic thyroid cancer. Amiodarone causes two types: iodine-induced overproduction (type 1) and destructive thyroiditis (type 2). Lithium mostly causes hypothyroidism and goitre by blocking release, but also silent thyroiditis.

Critical illness changes how T4 is converted and how the hypothalamus drives TSH, so the tests look hypothyroid while the gland is normal. This top-up is linked to 10 questions, more than any card in the deck.
    A septic or post-operative patient has a low T3 with a normal or low TSH - what is happening, and what do you do?
    • Cytokines, cortisol and fasting cut type 1 deiodinase (less T4 to T3) and raise type 3 (more reverse T3), so T3 falls first; type 2 changes are inconsistent.
    • Hypothalamic TRH falls, so TSH is normal or low: an acquired, transient central hypothyroidism (dopamine and steroids suppress TSH further).
    • Low T3 alone = mild illness; low T4 with low TSH = severe (total T4 < 4 µg/dL carries about 50% mortality).
    • In recovery TSH may rise transiently, usually below 20: this is not primary hypothyroidism.
    • Do not screen TFTs or give thyroxine: treat the illness, feed the patient, recheck 4–6 weeks after recovery; treat only clear primary disease (TSH > 20 with low free T4).
    Why, part by part
    • a Cytokines, cortisol and fasting cut type 1 and 2 deiodinase (less T4 to T3) and raise type 3 (more reverse T3), so T3 falls first. IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α act on the deiodinases, so the mechanism the questions want is inhibition of deiodinase activity, not more binding or more TSH. The firmest changes are less type 1 activity in the liver and more type 3 in liver and muscle; type 2 changes are inconsistent, and in some tissues it rises. Low T3 with high reverse T3 is the earliest and most consistent finding, and it may save energy during illness.
    • b Hypothalamic TRH falls, so TSH is normal or low: an acquired, transient CENTRAL hypothyroidism (dopamine and steroids suppress TSH further). Cytokines suppress both TRH and the pituitary’s response, so either answer may be keyed. Dopamine and high-dose steroids, common in the ICU, lower TSH further.
    • c Low T3 alone = mild illness; low T4 with low TSH = severe (total T4 <4 µg/dL carries about 50% mortality). The degree of abnormality follows the severity of illness: total T4 under 4 µg/dL carries up to about 50% mortality and under 2 µg/dL about 80%. A low T4 marks prognosis, not a thyroid problem to treat; the association does not show that the low T4 causes the deaths.
    • d In recovery TSH can overshoot to 10–20 mIU/L; this is not primary hypothyroidism. As the hypothalamus recovers, TSH rises above normal for a time to bring T4 back, usually staying below 20, so a mildly raised TSH in a recovering patient needs a repeat, not thyroxine; a TSH over 20 makes true primary hypothyroidism more likely.
    • e Do not screen TFTs or give thyroxine: treat the illness, feed the patient, recheck 4–6 weeks after recovery; treat only clear primary disease (TSH >20 with low free T4). Trials of thyroid hormone in non-thyroidal illness have not shown benefit. Routine testing in the ICU mainly creates false diagnoses, which is why testing should follow clinical suspicion. Some sources advise waiting at least 6 weeks after discharge, because an earlier test can catch the recovery rise.

    Thyroid Thyroid storm
    Storm is a trigger acting on a primed heart. Score it clinically, then block the tissue, stop synthesis, close release an hour later and cut conversion.
    The concept, in order
    1. Storm is thyrotoxicosis that has decompensated into organ failure. Hormone levels are no higher than in ordinary thyrotoxicosis, so no test makes the diagnosis.
    2. What tips it is a trigger acting on tissue with up-regulated β₁-receptors: infection in the undiagnosed, stopping the thionamide in the treated; surgery, labour and DKA too. Mortality is still about 1 in 10.
    3. Four features mark storm over plain thyrotoxicosis: fever (hyperpyrexia, the cardinal sign), a changed mind, a failing heart (AF, high-output failure) and a yellow liver.
    4. Score it: Burch–Wartofsky ≥ 45 = storm, 25–44 = impending, < 25 unlikely; ≥ 104 °F and seizure or coma score 30 each. The Japanese criteria need proven thyrotoxicosis and count organ failures (38 °C, pulse 130, bilirubin > 3).
    5. Treat at four points, in order. β-blocker first (propranolol): it acts in minutes on the primed heart and also cuts T4→T3.
    6. Then a thionamide: PTU is preferred in storm because it also blocks conversion (any trimester); methimazole 60–80 mg/day is acceptable. Give one thionamide, not both.
    7. Then iodine at least 1 h after the thionamide to shut off release. Given first, iodine becomes substrate for new hormone. Lithium replaces it when iodine cannot be used.
    8. Hydrocortisone (300 mg, then 100 mg 8-hourly): its keyed role is to cut T4→T3; it also covers adrenal reserve. Add paracetamol, not aspirin, cooling and fluids, and treat the trigger. No response in 24–48 h: plasma exchange, then thyroidectomy.
    Key vs guideline
    • Iodine timing: ATA 2016 says at least 1 h after the thionamide (the exam answer); JTA 2016 gives it at the same time.
    • Which β-blocker: the exam key is propranolol; JTA 2016 prefers β₁-selective landiolol or esmolol. Storm with AF and severe heart failure: DocT keys digoxin plus a cautious β-blocker (esmolol is equally supported); never full-dose propranolol or diltiazem.
    • Cards fixed: iodine is part of the first-line regimen (not ‘second line’); methimazole is 60–80 mg/day.

    The concept, in order
    1. Myxoedema coma is severe hypothyroidism plus a changed mind; most patients are obtunded rather than comatose. It is very rare, and mortality is 20–50% even with treatment.
    2. The background is long-standing hypothyroidism (Hashimoto’s, radioiodine or thyroidectomy, TKIs, lithium). The tip is infection, cold, stroke or sedatives and opioids, which these patients clear slowly.
    3. Recognise the pattern: hypothermia (< 35 °C), hypoventilation, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia and bradycardia, often in an elderly patient in winter. Fever may be absent even with sepsis.
