# GI01A review: independent fact-check (20 Nov 2026)

I checked all 14 cards for accuracy, fidelity (the bold restatements and letters match the cards), depth and rules. `check_learn.py GI01A` reports OK. There are no Top-up cards. The writer's file was sound and well sourced. Most changes settle the points the writer could not verify. Every trap was kept, and the truth side of each was checked.

## Changes
- **ALF-02 c:** the file said EASL 2017 uses "hyperacute ~1 week, acute 1–4 weeks, subacute 4–12 weeks". It now gives EASL's own wording: within 7 days of jaundice, 8–28 days, and 5–12 weeks. These match the card's O'Grady row (EASL 2017 PDF, easl.eu). The header line was corrected to match. **Factual (minor).**
- **ALF-03 a:** the ICH risk factors were listed without numbers, and said "vasopressor need". It now says renal impairment, inotrope need, and a persistent arterial ammonia above 200 µmol/L. It adds that EASL considers ICP monitoring when ammonia stays above 150–200 µmol/L despite fluids and RRT (EASL 2017). Depth.
- **ALF-07 c:** "EASL calls it [HBV] the commonest viral cause" was given as a worldwide fact. It now says:
  - EASL says this in a European setting.
  - Patterson (BMJ Open 2020, a systematic review of viral ALF) found hepatitis E commonest: about 32%, against 21% for HAV and 20% for HBV.
  - Hepatitis B stays the expected exam answer.
  - FLAG added. **Factual.**
- **ALF-07 d:** the UK 57% was left unexplained. It now says UK figures run from the high 50s to about 70%, depending on the centre and era. A UK transplant series gave 61.2% (PMC2742613). The Acharya 2002 East–West review gave about 70% for the UK and about 20% for the USA (J Gastroenterol Hepatol abstract). ALFSG 39% (Ostapowicz 2002) and the later 46% were kept. Settled as far as possible.
- **ALF-10 a:** added that in Lee 2009, the trial behind ACG's suggestion of NAC in non-paracetamol ALF, the benefit was confined to coma grades 1–2. Depth.
- **ALF-10 g:** said the fetal fatty-acid oxidation defect is present "in many cases". It now says about one in five (fetal LCHAD deficiency, Ibdah 1999; from standard references, not re-fetched). **Factual (minor).**
- **ALF-10 h:** added the Korman ratios (ALFSG, Hepatology 2008; 140 ALF patients, 16 with Wilson disease):
  - ALP (IU/L) : total bilirubin (mg/dL) below 4: sensitivity 94%, specificity 96%.
  - AST:ALT above 2.2: 94% and 86%.
  - The two together: 100% for both.
  - Caeruloplasmin: only 21% sensitive.
  - Kept the AASLD 2011 bilirubin (mg/dL) : ALP (IU/L) above 2, which I confirmed in the AASLD PDF.
  - Depth; settled.
- **ALF-10 extra (HBV prophylaxis):** "at least 6 months" now also says at least 12 months after anti-CD20 drugs (AASLD 2018 HBV guidance). Minor.
- **ALF-12 a:** "persistently high arterial ammonia (above about 200 µmol/L) is linked with herniation" was attributed loosely. It now says:
  - In Clemmesen 1999, patients who herniated had a mean arterial ammonia of 230 ± 58 µmol/L, against 118 ± 48 in those who did not. This is the origin of the usual 200 µmol/L level (abstract).
  - EASL treats a persistent ammonia above 200 µmol/L as high risk.
  - AASLD 2011 quotes the same 200 cut-off, but prints the unit as µg/dL.
  - Settled.
- **ALF-13 d:** added the guideline intervals, and a FLAG:
  - EASL 2017: neurological checks and glucose about every 2 hours (glucose 2-hourly in hyperacute cases), and hourly urine output.
  - AASLD 2011: hourly neurological checks in advanced encephalopathy without an ICP monitor; aminotransferases and bilirubin daily.
  - So the card's 2–4 hours is the slow end. The card's 12–24 hours for laboratory tests matches.
  - **Factual (card).**
- **Header:** added the reviewer's sources with dates.

## Settled points (the writer's open questions)
- **Trey & Davidson (1970):** encephalopathy within 8 weeks of the first symptom. **Bernuau (1986):** under 2 weeks (fulminant) and 2–12 weeks (subfulminant), counted from jaundice. **O'Grady (1993):** up to 7 days, 8–28 days and 5–12 weeks, counted from jaundice. EASL 2017 quotes these intervals word for word. The card is correct.
- **Japanese criteria (J Gastroenterol 2012, Springer full text):** the intervals are counted from the onset of disease symptoms, not from jaundice.
  - Acute type: within 10 days. Subacute type: 11–56 days.
  - Late-onset hepatic failure: 8–24 weeks.
  - PT 40% or less, or INR 1.5 or more.
  - The writer's ALF-02 FLAG is correct.
- **Clemmesen ammonia threshold:** see ALF-12 a. The abstract gives group means, not a cut-off. 200 µmol/L is the derived level, and EASL and AASLD both use it.
- **Korman Wilson ratios:** confirmed. ALP:bilirubin below 4 (94%/96%) and AST:ALT above 2.2 (94%/86%); combined, 100%/100%.
- **Aetiology shares:**
  - USA: 39% (Ostapowicz 2002), about 46% in later ALFSG data.
  - UK: the published range is about 57–70%.
  - India: paracetamol essentially 0%.
  - The Bangladesh and Sudan figures on the watch line were not traced.
- **Monitoring frequencies:** see ALF-13 d.
- **AIIMS pregnancy data (Bhatia et al., Hepatology 2008):** confirmed by the writer.
  - 1,015 patients, 249 of them pregnant.
  - Hepatitis E caused 59.4% of cases in pregnancy.
  - Mortality did not differ.
- **The truth side of the traps:**
  - AIH steroid failure at 7 days means listing (EASL: "lack of improvement within seven days should lead to listing").
  - Budd–Chiari: transplant "provided malignancy is excluded" (AASLD).
  - Factor V logic.
  - HEV IgM can be falsely negative.
  - Amanita timing.
  - Aflatoxin is not an acute toxin.
  - All of these are correct.

## Not verified
- The ALF-08 aetiology percentage table (41.8, 31.1, 10.3 and so on) was not found in the Bhatia 2008 abstract. The writer's FLAG stands.
- The Bangladesh (hepatitis E 75%) and Sudan (unknown 38%, hepatitis B 22%) figures on the ALF-07 watch line.
- Ibdah 1999 (about one in five) is from memory and was not re-fetched.

## Final FLAGS
- **ALF-02:** the Japanese row and the watch line are wrong. The Japanese criteria count from the onset of symptoms, not from jaundice. So Trey & Davidson is not the only system that counts from the first symptom.
- **ALF-06:** the card is correct. The Marrow key (Budd–Chiari ALF two months after colon cancer: evaluate for transplant) conflicts with AASLD's "provided malignancy is excluded".
- **ALF-07:**
  - c: hepatitis B as the commonest virus worldwide is standard teaching. The 2020 systematic review found hepatitis E commonest.
  - d: the USA 39% is the 2002 ALFSG figure; later data give about 46%. The UK 57% is one of a range of 57–70%, and its source was not traced.
- **ALF-08:** the 2008 Hepatology paper is Bhatia, Singhal, Panda and Acharya, with 1,015 patients, not "about 1,100". The aetiology table was not traced to it.
- **ALF-10:** the card is correct. The hepatitis B QBank key (antiviral first) is defensible, but transplant evaluation is also right at MELD 18. AASLD 2011 says only that antivirals "may be considered" in acute HBV ALF.
- **ALF-11:** a, "First sign: encephalopathy", should read "the defining sign". The prodrome and jaundice usually come first.
- **ALF-13 (new):** d, "clinical every 2–4 hours", is slower than the guidelines: EASL every 2 hours, AASLD hourly in advanced encephalopathy without an ICP monitor.
