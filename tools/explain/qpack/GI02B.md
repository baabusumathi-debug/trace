## PANC-14 · Fluid resuscitation in acute — 2 questions

- **[DocT GT · CGT2]** A 40-year-old female presents with epigastric pain radiating to back. Serum lipase elevated. After 48 hrs, she develops hypotension, hypoxemia, and rising creatinine. CT shows >50% pancreatic necrosis.  What is the most important early management strategy?
  - Options: A) Prophylactic antibiotics || B) Early aggressive IV crystalloid || C) Immediate surgical necrosectomy || D) TPN
  - Answer: B · Early aggressive IV crystalloid
  - key_note: Keyed 'early aggressive crystalloid' is the best of these options, but WATERFALL (2022) showed aggressive fluids (20 mL/kg bolus + 3 mL/kg/h) caused more fluid overload with no benefit. Current practice is moderate, goal-directed Ringer's lactate, as the card says.
- **[DocT GT · AIM1]** A 50-year-old man with severe pancreatitis requires fluid resuscitation. Which is the preferred early approach?
  - Options: A) 3 mL/kg/h Ringer lactate || B) 0.9% saline boluses aggressively || C) Restrict fluids until electrolytes normalize || D) TPN over enteral feeding first 48h
  - Answer: A · 3 mL/kg/h Ringer lactate · cohort right 69%
  - line: DocT: Give goal-directed Ringer's lactate at a moderate rate (DocT keys about 3 mL/kg/h; WATERFALL 2022 used 1.5 mL/kg/h plus a 10 mL/kg bolus only if hypovolaemic). Reassess at 3, 12 and 24 h against urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h and falling haematocrit and BUN. Aggressive saline boluses cause fluid overload without benefit.
  - key_note: WATERFALL moderate arm was 1.5 mL/kg/h; 3 mL/kg/h is still the best of the options.

## PANC-18 · Antibiotics in acute pancreatitis: — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** A 72 year old male with severe acute pancreatitis develops necrotizing pancreatitis and suspected pancreatic infection, indicated by gas in the pancreatic bed on CT scan and positive Gram stain for Gram-negative rods from a fine-needle aspirate. He is hypotensive and oliguric. His previous cultures from a distant wound showed an ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae. Considering the empirical guidelines and the principles of the MERINO trial, which of the following is the most appropriate initial antimicrobial therapy?
  - Options: A) Piperacillin-Tazobactam. || B) Cefepime. || C) Meropenem. || D) Ciprofloxacin.
  - Answer: C · Meropenem. · cohort right 72%
  - line: DocT: MERINO (JAMA 2018; ceftriaxone-resistant E. coli/Klebsiella bacteraemia): 30-day mortality 12.3% on pip-tazo vs 3.7% on meropenem, so pip-tazo is not a carbapenem substitute (inoculum effect, co-carried OXA-1). Carbapenems also penetrate infected pancreatic necrosis.
  - pattern: same-number: MERINO 12.3% vs 3.7% (pip-tazo vs meropenem); not to be confused with other carbapenem-vs-BLI trials
- **[Marrow QBank]** Day 7, gas in pancreatic necrosis: best initial antibiotic
  - Answer: A · Imipenem
  - note: Carbapenem + gas-in-collection sign.
  - topic: Infected necrosis · carbapenem

On day 2, he develops progressive abdominal distension, reduced urine output, and worsening ventilator compliance.His bladder pressure is 21 mmHg, consistent with intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH). Arterial blood gas shows mild respiratory acidosis, and his plateau pressures are elevated.Which of the following ventilatory strategies is preferred in this patient ?

## PANC-22 · Necrotising pancreatitis: the step-up — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** Which of the following statements regarding the PANTER trial in necrotising pancreatitis is true?
  - Options: A) Early necrosectomy improves outcomes || B) Step-up approach reduces morbidity || C) Open necrosectomy has superior outcomes || D) Antibiotics are contraindicated in sterile necrosis
  - Answer: B · Step-up approach reduces morbidity · cohort right 51%
  - pattern: lookalike: infected pancreatic necrosis = DELAY and step up (wait ~4 weeks to wall off, drain first) vs every other intra-abdominal sepsis = source control within 6 h (SSC 2026).
- **[DocT QBank]** Which of the following statements best summarises the findings of the TENSION trial in the management of necrotising pancreatitis?
  - Options: A) Early surgical necrosectomy improves mortality || B) Conservative management is inferior to open necrosectomy || C) Endoscopic step-up approach is associated with fewer complications || D) Antibiotics alone are sufficient in all necrotic pancreatitis cases
  - Answer: C · Endoscopic step-up approach is associated with fewer complications · cohort right 79%
  - line: DocT: TENSION (Lancet 2018; 98 infected-necrosis patients): endoscopic step-up vs surgical step-up gave the same major complications or death (43% vs 45%), but fewer pancreatic fistulas (5% vs 32%) and a shorter stay. So PANTER = step-up beats open necrosectomy; TENSION = endoscopic step-up equals surgical step-up, with fewer fistulas.
  - key_note: 'Fewer complications' overstates it: the primary composite (major complications or death) did not differ; only pancreatic fistula and length of stay favoured endoscopy. Option C is still the best of those offered.

## PANC-23 · Indications to intervene on a — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** A patient recovering from severe necrotising pancreatitis continues to have high-volume pancreatic fluid drainage from a percutaneous drain. CT reveals a disconnected pancreatic tail. What is the most appropriate definitive management?
  - Options: A) Octreotide therapy || B) Distal pancreatectomy || C) ERCP with stenting || D) TPN and observation
  - Answer: B · Distal pancreatectomy · cohort right 72%
  - line: DocT: Disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome (viable tail cut off from the head, giving a persistent high-output external fistula or recurrent collections) cannot be bridged by a transpapillary stent. First line now is EUS-guided transmural drainage with long-term indwelling plastic stents; distal pancreatectomy is the definitive surgery when that fails, and octreotide only lowers output.
  - key_note: Distal pancreatectomy is the classic 'definitive' answer; current practice tries EUS-guided transmural drainage with indwelling stents first, but that option is not offered, so the key stands.
- **[DocT QBank]** A 65-year-old man with acute necrotising pancreatitis develops a high-output drain with amylase-rich fluid. An ERCP image is shown.   What is the most appropriate next step in management?
  - Options: A) Continue antibiotics || B) External drainage only || C) Pancreatic duct stenting || D) Surgical necrosectomy
  - Answer: C · Pancreatic duct stenting · cohort right 57%
  - line: DocT: A PARTIAL duct leak (high-amylase drain fluid, pancreatic ascites or effusion, duct still in continuity) is treated by ERCP with a transpapillary pancreatic stent that bridges the leak, turning an external fistula into internal drainage. A stent cannot bridge a completely disconnected duct, which is the syndrome on this card.
  - key_note: Image file not available locally; judged from stem and explanation.
