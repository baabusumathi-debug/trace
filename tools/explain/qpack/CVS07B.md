## PCA-28 · Trace how TTM practice changed across the evidence: — 3 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** In therapeutic hypothermia, what is the recommended target temperature for patients following out-of-hospital ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest?
  - Options: A) 36 °37°C || B) 34 ° 36°C || C) 32 ° 34°C || D) 30 ° 32°C
  - Answer: C · 32 ° 34°C · cohort right 57%
  - key_note: Key C (32–34 °C × 12–24 h) is the 2002–2010 AHA answer. After TTM (33 vs 36: no difference) and TTM2 (≤33 vs ≤37.5: no difference, more arrhythmia), AHA 2025 recommends temperature control 32–37.5 °C for ≥36 h in any comatose post-arrest patient, any rhythm; ERC/ESICM 2021: prevent fever (≤37.7 °C) ≥72 h. If forced, pick the range the exam asks for; know 32–34 is historical.
- **[DocT QBank]** Which of the following statements about therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is correct?
  - Options: A) TH is indicated for all patients with cardiac arrest || B) The primary temperature goal of TH is 32°C to 36°C for 48 hours || C) Shivering is a desired effect during the cooling phase of TH || D) Delaying the initiation of TH increases mortality
  - Answer: D · Delaying the initiation of TH increases mortality · cohort right 47%
  - line: DocT: No RCT shows that earlier cooling lowers mortality: prehospital cold-saline cooling (Kim, JAMA 2014, n=1,359; RINSE) gave no survival or neurological benefit and caused more re-arrest and pulmonary oedema. DocT's key D rests on older observational data; all four options are flawed by 2025 standards.
  - key_note: All four options are wrong by 2025 standards: A (not all arrests — comatose survivors only), B (24 h not 48 h; range now 32–37.5), C (shivering is harmful), D (no RCT shows time-to-target matters; prehospital cooling trials negative). D is DocT's key based on older observational data.

## PCA-29 · For the practical conduct of TTM, give: — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** Which of the following physiological principles best explains the rationale for a slow and controlled rewarming rate in a patient who has undergone therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest?
  - Options: A) Rapid rewarming abruptly increases cerebral metabolic demand, potentially exceeding oxygen delivery and causing secondary brain injury. || B) Slow rewarming allows for gradual restoration of enzymatic function and reduces the risk of precipitating malignant hyperthermia. || C) Controlled rewarming minimises the risk of a rapid decrease in serum potassium and the development of post-rewarming diuresis. || D) Rapid rewarming can lead to a paradoxical decrease in core temperature due to peripheral vasodilation and continued heat loss from the core.
  - Answer: A · Rapid rewarming abruptly increases cerebral metabolic demand, poten... · cohort right 45%
  - line: DocT: Rewarm after TTM at ≤0.25–0.5 °C/h because fast rewarming abruptly raises cerebral metabolic demand in a brain whose oxygen delivery is still fragile (secondary injury); it also causes vasodilatory hypotension and REBOUND HYPERkalaemia as K+ moves back out of cells (option C has the direction wrong). Option D describes afterdrop, an accidental-hypothermia phenomenon.
- **[DocT GT · GT7]** Which of the following statements about therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is correct?
  - Options: A) TH is indicated for all patients with cardiac arrest || B) The primary temperature goal of TH is 32°C to 36°C for 48 hours || C) Shivering is a desired effect during the cooling phase of TH. || D) Delaying the initiation of TH increases mortality.
  - Answer: D · Delaying the initiation of TH increases mortality. · cohort right 50%
  - line: DocT: Temperature control is for comatose survivors after ROSC (not all arrests); targeted hypothermia is held for 24 h (not 48), shivering is unwanted and must be suppressed, and earlier initiation is linked to better outcome. Current ERC-ESICM practice is active fever prevention (≤37.5-37.7 °C) for at least 72 h.
  - pattern: D is the only defensible option, but 'every hour of delay raises mortality by 20%' comes from observational data; prehospital cooling trials showed no benefit and TTM2 found no gain from 33 °C over normothermia.

## PCA-34 · For the advanced temperature package and Tier-1 anti-shivering, give: — 3 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** A patient undergoing therapeutic hypothermia post-cardiac arrest develops shivering. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial intervention to manage this shivering while minimising potential adverse effects on neurological assessment and hemodynamic stability?
  - Options: A) Administer a bolus of cold intravenous saline to enhance cooling further and suppress thermogenesis || B) Initiate neuromuscular blockade with continuous electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring to abolish muscle activity directly || C) Apply skin counter warming measures and consider a magnesium sulfate infusion while closely monitoring core temperature || D) Administer a single dose of a short-acting opioid to reduce metabolic demand and indirectly decrease shivering
  - Answer: C · Apply skin counter warming measures and consider a magnesium sulfat... · cohort right 32%
  - line: DocT: Skin counter-warming plus magnesium is the first answer for TTM shivering because neither sedates nor drops BP. Opioid boluses (D) are a later, sedating tier, cold saline (A) worsens shivering, and paralysis (B) is the last resort, with EEG.
- **[DocT GT · GT3]** A 62-year-old man is undergoing targeted temperature management (TTM) after cardiac arrest. He develops moderate shivering despite passive rewarming. The team considers pharmacologic strategies. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to manage his shivering while minimizing risk of hypotension and excessive sedation?
  - Options: A) Initiate a continuous infusion of midazolam || B) Administer a bolus of ketamine || C) Begin dexmedetomidine infusion || D) Administer IV magnesium sulfate
  - Answer: C · Begin dexmedetomidine infusion · cohort right 71%
  - key_note: The Columbia anti-shivering ladder puts magnesium (with buspirone, paracetamol and skin counter-warming) before dexmedetomidine, and magnesium has the least hypotension and sedation, so D is at least as defensible as C for 'minimising hypotension and sedation'. Dexmedetomidine is the next tier.
