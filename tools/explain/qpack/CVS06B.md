## RVF-23 · Give the clinical signs of RV failure under: — 1 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** A 65-year-old male with a history of coronary artery disease presents with acute shortness of breath, hypotension, and a swollen, distended abdomen. His blood pressure is 85/45 mmHg, heart rate is 100 bpm, and CVP is 20 mmHg. His echocardiogram shows a severely dilated right ventricle. What is the most likely diagnosis?
  - Options: A) Right-sided heart failure due to pulmonary hypertension || B) Acute pericardial tamponade || C) Left-sided heart failure with pulmonary edema || D) Massive pulmonary embolism
  - Answer: A · Right-sided heart failure due to pulmonary hypertension · cohort right 67%
  - line: DocT: Dilated RV + CVP 20 + abdominal distension keys to CHRONIC RV failure from pulmonary hypertension, not massive PE. PE dilates the RV abruptly (McConnell sign, 60/60 sign) with no time for ascites to build, and tamponade gives small, collapsed right chambers.
  - key_note: Defensible but ambiguous: acute dyspnoea + hypotension + dilated RV also fits massive PE (D); the abdominal distension (chronic congestion) is what tips it to A.
  - pattern: lookalike: acute PE vs chronic PH — RV wall thickness >5 mm dates it as chronic

## RVF-36 · Give the five pillars of acute RV failure management, with their contents: — 3 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT2]** A 45-year-old woman with idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) is admitted with right heart failure on IV epoprostenol. Which of the following is the most appropriate additional therapy?
  - Options: A) Lifelong anticoagulation || B) Routine digoxin therapy || C) Rapid aggressive diuresis || D) Calcium channel blocker trial
  - Answer: A · Lifelong anticoagulation · cohort right 64%
  - line: DocT: In IPAH with acute RV failure on IV epoprostenol: never interrupt the prostacyclin (rebound PH crisis), optimise preload cautiously (loop diuretic, avoid rapid aggressive diuresis), keep systemic BP. Routine lifelong anticoagulation is no longer recommended (ESC/ERS 2022: individualise, e.g. indwelling-catheter thrombosis risk), and CCB vasoreactivity trials are for stable outpatients only.
  - key_note: Key A rests on the older practice of anticoagulating IPAH (and catheter thrombosis on epoprostenol). ESC/ERS 2022 makes no general recommendation for anticoagulation in IPAH; A is only the least-wrong option.

## RVF-46 · For mechanical circulatory support, give: — 1 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT1o]** A 40-year-old male with known nonischemic cardiomyopathy and pulmonary hypertension returns to the ICU intubated and sedated after undergoing an orthotopic heart transplant. Graft ischemia time was 4 hours. On arrival, he is on high-dose intravenous vasopressor support including 0.5 μg/kg/min norepinephrine, 0.5 μg/kg/min epinephrine, and 0.1 units/h of vasopressin. He is receiving inhaled nitric oxide 40 ppm. Monitoring reveals the following: Cardiac index of 1.6 L/min/m2, invasive arterial pressure of 80/40 mm Hg, central venous pressure of 18 mm Hg, and heart rate 130 beats/min. The LEAST likely cause of his cardiogenic shock is:
  - Options: A) T-lymphocyte-mediated rejection of allograft || B) Mediastinal bleeding with regional cardiac tamponade || C) Ischemia-reperfusion injury-related primary graft failure || D) Acute on chronic pulmonary hypertension
  - Answer: A · T-lymphocyte-mediated rejection of allograft · cohort right 23%
  - line: DocT: Shock within hours of a heart transplant has three main causes: primary graft dysfunction (ischaemia-reperfusion, long ischaemic time), RV failure against the recipient's pulmonary hypertension, and tamponade from mediastinal bleeding. T-cell (acute cellular) rejection is the least likely: it takes days to weeks. Only hyperacute, antibody-mediated rejection strikes within minutes to hours.
