## PLEU-16 · Ultrasound and CT for pleural — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** During POCUS of a patient with sepsis, a dependent anechoic pleural collection moves sinusoidally with respiration. What does this indicate?
  - Options: A) Highly viscous empyema || B) Simple transudative effusion || C) Organized hemothorax || D) Consolidated lung parenchyma
  - Answer: B · Simple transudative effusion · cohort right 61%
  - line: DocT: The sinusoid sign (M-mode: the lung line swings toward the pleural line on inspiration) proves FREE-FLOWING, low-viscosity fluid — a simple effusion — but it cannot prove a transudate. Septated or viscous collections (empyema, organised haemothorax) damp the swing.
  - key_note: Keyed B is plainly the best option (A, C, D are wrong); 'transudative' is an over-read — the sinusoid sign shows free-flowing fluid, and anechoic fluid can be exudate. Flag removed.
- **[NEET 2024 recall]** Sinusoid sign on lung US
  - Answer: Pleural effusion

## PLEU-24 · Haemothorax: — 1 questions

- **[DocT GT · GrandT1]** A 72-year-old man presents to the emergency department with sudden onset right-sided chest pain and dyspnea following a mechanical fall. He takes apixaban for atrial fibrillation. On examination, he is hypotensive with decreased breath sounds on the right. Chest CT without contrast reveals a large right-sided pleural effusion with a Hounsfield unit (HU) measurement of 35. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
  - Options: A) Transudative pleural effusion || B) Chylothorax || C) Hemothorax || D) Empyema
  - Answer: C · Hemothorax · cohort right 84%
  - line: DocT: CT density sorts pleural fluid: transudate ~0–15 HU; >15–20 HU suggests exudate or blood; fresh haemothorax 30–45 HU (clot 50–90 HU). Dense effusion + hypotension after a fall on apixaban = haemothorax until proven otherwise (confirm with fluid Hct >50% of blood).

## PLEU-26 · Re-expansion pulmonary oedema: — 2 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT2o]** You are the sole intensivist at a rural hospital, and the overnight hospitalist asked you to evaluate a patient with a left-sided malignant pleural effusion who is experiencing dyspnea. You determine that the patient has a very large pleural effusion, and you decide that a thoracentesis will be needed. Under ultrasound guidance, you place a chest tube and begin to aspirate the pleural effusion. At what point should you stop your thoracentesis to maximize your drainage and minimize complication(s)?
  - Options: A) Stop after draining 1 L of pleural effusion || B) Stop after patient complains of chest discomfort || C) Stop after your pleural manometer goes below -20 cm H2O || D) Stop after patient coughs
  - Answer: B · Stop after patient complains of chest discomfort · cohort right 11%
  - line: DocT: Stop a therapeutic thoracentesis when the patient develops chest discomfort or pressure (BTS 2023) — symptoms track a steep fall in pleural pressure better than volume. Draining more than 1–1.5 L is safe if asymptomatic, and stopping at −20 cmH2O on manometry has not been shown to prevent re-expansion oedema.

  - Answer: B · Reexpansion pulmonary oedema after drainage · cohort right 88%
