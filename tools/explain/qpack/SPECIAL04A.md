## PK-01 · Pharmacokinetics in the critically ill: — 3 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT1o]** A 52-year-old woman is admitted to the ICU, who is s/p orthotopic liver transplant 2 weeks ago, for septic shock requiring vasopressor support. Pertinent past medical history includes an unprovoked pulmonary embolism 3 months ago for which she takes apixaban 5 mg twice daily at home. Review of systems reveals normal renal function, active bowel sounds with two recent bowel movements, and a Glasgow Coma Score of 15. In the presence of distributive shock requiring vasopressor support, with regard to absorption, which scheduled medications would you be most concerned about?
  - Options: A) Subcutaneous enoxaparin || B) Sublingual tacrolimus || C) Intravenous hydrocortisone || D) Oral mycophenolate
  - Answer: A · Subcutaneous enoxaparin · cohort right 35%
  - line: DocT: Shock and vasopressors reduce skin perfusion, so SUBCUTANEOUS drugs are absorbed poorly. SC enoxaparin gives lower anti-Xa levels in patients on vasopressors; consider UFH by IV infusion. Sublingual, IV and oral routes (with a working gut) are less affected.
- **[DocT GT · GT1o]** A 76-year-old woman is admitted to the ICU for atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate after an exploratory laparotomy. You elect to use Drug A for rate control. The oral formulation of Drug A undergoes significant first-pass metabolism. With this knowledge, what dosage adjustment should be made to the intravenous form of Drug A?
  - Options: A) Increase the dose || B) Decrease the dose || C) Make no dosage adjustment || D) Increase the dosing interval
  - Answer: B · Decrease the dose · cohort right 31%
  - line: DocT: Drugs with high first-pass metabolism (metoprolol, propranolol, diltiazem, verapamil, morphine) need a much SMALLER IV dose than oral dose, because IV bypasses gut-wall and hepatic extraction. Example: metoprolol 5 mg IV is roughly equal to 25-50 mg orally.
- **[DocT GT · GT1o]** A 43-year-old woman is admitted to the ICU with communityacquired pneumonia. She is mechanically intubated and requires vasopressor support. Pertinent past medical history includes seizure disorder for which she takes phenytoin 100 mg by mouth three times daily. On day 3 in the ICU, patient remains intubated, is off vasopressors, and enteral tube feeding is started. Pertinent lab values on day 3 are as follows: ALT 154 U/L, AST 95 U/L, albumin 1.5 g/dL, SCr 2.3 mg/dL. On day 5 in the ICU patient suffers a seizure. What is the most likely cause of her seizure?
  - Options: A) Kidney dysfunction || B) Enteral tube feeds || C) Elevated liver enzymes || D) Hypoalbuminemia
  - Answer: B · Enteral tube feeds · cohort right 20%
  - key_note: Enteral feeds bind phenytoin and cut its absorption, so levels fall and seizures follow. Hold feeds 1-2 h before and after each dose, or give IV. Hypoalbuminaemia does the opposite: it raises FREE phenytoin and risks toxicity, so check a free level.

- **[NEET 2025 recall]** A patient with a history of multiple substance abuse is brought to the ICU with altered mental status and suspected poisoning. Which of the following changes in pharmacokinetic parameters is most likely to occur in critically ill poisoned patients and complicate their management?
  - Options: A) Increased first-pass metabolism || B) Decreased volume of distribution of hydrophilic drugs || C) Reduced hepatic enzyme activity || D) Alterations in plasma protein binding
  - Answer: D · Alterations in plasma protein binding
  - line: DocT: The PK change that most complicates poisoned/critically ill patients is altered protein binding: hypoalbuminaemia and acidosis raise the free fraction of highly bound drugs (phenytoin, valproate, warfarin), so total levels mislead. ## PK-05 · Hypoalbuminaemia and drug levels: — 4 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT2]** A 60-year-old male undergoing hemodialysis is prescribed phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis. His total phenytoin level is 10 mg/L. Despite being within the therapeutic range, he shows signs of toxicity. Which of the following best explains this finding?
  - Options: A) Increased hepatic clearance || B) Elevated albumin levels || C) Increased fraction unbound || D) Decreased bioavailability
  - Answer: C · Increased fraction unbound · cohort right 100%
  - line: DocT: Phenytoin is ~90% albumin-bound with a narrow window; in uraemia/ESRD (displacement by uraemic toxins) and hypoalbuminaemia the free fraction rises from 10% to 20-30%, so a 'therapeutic' total level (10-20 mg/L) can be toxic. Measure free phenytoin (target 1-2 mg/L) or correct: Winter-Tozer total ÷ (0.2 × albumin g/dL + 0.1), using 0.1 × albumin in ESRD.
- **[DocT GT · GT3]** Which drug characteristic makes protein binding changes most clinically relevant? (fu is the fraction unbound)
  - Options: A) High extraction, IV use || B) Low fu, oral use || C) High protein affinity || D) Low hepatic clearance
  - Answer: A · High extraction, IV use · cohort right 55%
  - line: DocT: A change in protein binding lastingly alters the FREE (active) concentration only for highly bound drugs that are high-extraction and given IV (e.g. IV lidocaine, fentanyl, propofol) or have a narrow therapeutic index. For low-extraction drugs (phenytoin, valproate), free levels return to normal as clearance of unbound drug rises; only the total level falls, which is an interpretation trap.
- **[DocT GT · GT4]** A 60-year-old male undergoing haemodialysis is prescribed phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis. His total phenytoin level is 10 mg/L. Despite being within the therapeutic range, he shows signs of toxicity. Which of the following best explains this finding?
  - Options: A) Increased hepatic clearance || B) Elevated albumin levels || C) Increased fraction unbound || D) Decreased bioavailability
  - Answer: C · Increased fraction unbound · cohort right 89%
  - line: DocT: Phenytoin is ~90% albumin-bound; in uraemia/dialysis (toxins displace it) or hypoalbuminaemia the unbound fraction rises, so a patient can be toxic at a 'therapeutic' total level of 10 mg/L. Measure FREE phenytoin (target 1–2 mg/L) or use a corrected level; do not raise the dose to chase the total.

## PK-08 · Antibiotics on renal replacement — 3 questions

- **[DocT GT · GT1o]** Which pathophysiological change seen in critically ill patients most frequently makes medication dosage adjustment necessary?
  - Options: A) Decreased plasma protein binding || B) Decreased renal clearance || C) Diminished GI or subcutaneous perfusion || D) Inhibition of hepatic enzymes
  - Answer: B · Decreased renal clearance · cohort right 60%
  - line: DocT: Reduced renal clearance (AKI or CKD) is the commonest reason to adjust drug doses in the ICU. Most antibiotics, LMWH and many opioid metabolites are renally eliminated and dosed by estimated CrCl (Cockcroft-Gault). Changes in protein binding, gut or SC perfusion, and hepatic enzymes matter for fewer drugs.

On ICU day 4, she is started on continuous veno-venous hemodialysis (CVVHD). You cannot find any dosing recommendations for CVVHD; however, you're able to find that this new drug is 60% renally eliminated as unchanged drug, volume of distribution is 3 L/kg, its ∼25% bound to protein, and has a small molecular weight.   - key_note: Key A is right. Trap: DocT's explanation implies a large Vd should limit removal. With continuous therapy, the added clearance comes from the free fraction and effluent rate, and Vd mainly matters for intermittent dialysis and rebound.
- **[DocT GT · GrandT1]** A 45-year-old ICU patient on continuous renal replacement therapy exhibits subtherapeutic vancomycin levels despite standard dosing. Which stewardship strategy best addresses this issue?
  - Options: A) Deescalation of empiric therapy || B) Therapeutic drug monitoring || C) Rapid microbiologic diagnostics || D) Closed multidisciplinary rounds
  - Answer: B · Therapeutic drug monitoring · cohort right 82%
  - line: DocT: Subtherapeutic vancomycin on CRRT (or with augmented renal clearance) is a dosing problem, so the stewardship fix is therapeutic drug monitoring: AUC-guided dosing (target AUC/MIC 400–600, Bayesian software, or trough-based if unavailable) after a 25–30 mg/kg load, adjusting for effluent rate. De-escalation, rapid diagnostics and rounds don't correct a low level.
