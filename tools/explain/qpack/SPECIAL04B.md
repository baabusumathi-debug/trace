## PK-18 · Glycopeptides and others: — 2 questions

- **[DocT QBank]** A 55 year old female with acute kidney injury (AKI) requiring hemodialysis develops a central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI). Blood cultures grow Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The patient is critically ill with septic shock. Considering the findings of the AVERT trial regarding optimization of therapy for specific infections, which of the following antimicrobial regimens is the most appropriate initial therapy, given her renal status?
  - Options: A) Vancomycin (loading dose followed by continuous infusion). || B) Daptomycin (daily dosing adjusted for hemodialysis || C) Linezolid (standard dosing with close monitoring || D) Ceftaroline (daily dosing adjusted for hemodialysis).
  - Answer: C · Linezolid (standard dosing with close monitoring · cohort right 49%
  - key_note: IDSA MRSA 2011 and later SAB guidance: vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA bacteraemia; linezolid only as alternative/salvage (bacteriostatic, thrombocytopenia). Renal failure on HD is handled by dosing vancomycin to levels or daptomycin q48h. B (daptomycin) or A (vancomycin) is better than C. The AVERT trial reference is irrelevant.
- **[DocT GT · GrandT1]** A 45-year-old ICU patient on continuous renal replacement therapy exhibits subtherapeutic vancomycin levels despite standard dosing. Which stewardship strategy best addresses this issue?
  - Options: A) Deescalation of empiric therapy || B) Therapeutic drug monitoring || C) Rapid microbiologic diagnostics || D) Closed multidisciplinary rounds
  - Answer: B · Therapeutic drug monitoring · cohort right 82%
  - line: DocT: Subtherapeutic vancomycin on CRRT (or with augmented renal clearance) is a dosing problem, so the stewardship fix is therapeutic drug monitoring: AUC-guided dosing (target AUC/MIC 400–600, Bayesian software, or trough-based if unavailable) after a 25–30 mg/kg load, adjusting for effluent rate. De-escalation, rapid diagnostics and rounds don't correct a low level.

## PK-20 · Antifungal penetration: — 2 questions

- **[DocT GT · CGT5]** A 65-year-old woman in the ICU develops symptomatic Candida urinary tract infection. Imaging shows no fungus ball or renal abscess. Which antifungal agent is preferred because it achieves high urinary concentrations?
  - Options: A) Caspofungin || B) Posaconazole || C) Fluconazole || D) Voriconazole
  - Answer: C · Fluconazole
- **[DocT GT · GT6]** Drug of choice for lower UTI caused by candida auris?
  - Options: A) Ampho B deoxycholate || B) Fluconazole || C) Anidulafungin || D) Posaconazole
  - Answer: C · Anidulafungin · cohort right 82%
  - key_note: Echinocandins achieve very low urine concentrations, so they are not reliable for Candida cystitis (the card's rule). For azole-resistant species such as C. auris, IDSA/CDC advise amphotericin B deoxycholate (0.3-0.6 mg/kg, ± bladder irrigation) or flucytosine for lower UTI; A is the better answer, though DocT reflects CDC's 'echinocandin first-line' for invasive C. auris.

## PK-21 · Antifungal PK/PD and levels: — 1 questions

- **[DocT GT · CGT2]** Which of the following is incorrect about Voriconazole?
  - Options: A) Oral Voriconazole is preferred in patients having renal dysfunction || B) IV Voriconazole is preferred in patients having renal dysfunction || C) It is first line treatment for Mucormycosis || D) It is fungicidal against Candida
  - Answer: A · Oral Voriconazole is preferred in patients having renal dysfunction
  - key_note: Keyed 'incorrect' option A is actually TRUE: oral voriconazole is preferred in renal dysfunction because the IV vehicle (SBECD cyclodextrin) accumulates, as the card states.
