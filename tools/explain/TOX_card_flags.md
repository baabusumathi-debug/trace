# Toxicology decks — where the card text may be wrong or outdated (5 Nov 2026)

Found while writing and independently fact-checking Explain v2 for TOX02–TOX06. Card text was **not** changed; the explanations give the correct version. Decide card by card whether to edit the card (in-app edit) or keep it.

## TOX02 · 7 flags

- **APAP-01** — WATCH says pesticides ‘dominate’ — true of poisoning in India, but the commonest causes of acute liver failure in India are viral hepatitis (HEV, HBV) and anti-tubercular drug injury, not pesticides. Consider rewording.
- **APAP-03** — Card groups anticonvulsants under ‘CYP2E1 inducers’; only isoniazid (and alcohol) act mainly on CYP2E1 — phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital and rifampicin induce other CYP isoforms. Evidence for inducers raising risk is weak (UK dropped the high-risk line in 2012). Explanation says ‘CYP inducers’.
- **APAP-04** — Repeated-ingestion dose thresholds vary by guideline and the card’s 7.5 g / 4 g in 24 h matches none exactly: US out-of-hospital consensus (Dart 2006) and Australia–NZ use ≥10 g or 200 mg/kg in 24 h, or ≥6 g or 150 mg/kg per 24 h for ≥48 h; Australia–NZ adds 4 g or 100 mg/kg in 24 h with risk factors. The level >20 µg/mL and ALT >50 triggers agree (Australia–NZ). Explanation states the range.
- **APAP-05** — Stage IV timing: Goldfrank places recovery from about day 4–5 to 2 weeks, with histological recovery up to 3 months; ‘usually complete by day 7’ is optimistic. Explanation adds this.
- **APAP-08** — Charcoal ‘within 4 hours’ is broader than most guidelines: the usual window is 1 hour; Australia–NZ uses 2 hours for a toxic immediate-release dose and up to 4 hours only for >30 g or modified-release ingestions. Explanation says this.
- **APAP-11** — Lactate modification (Bernal, Lancet 2002): >3.5 mmol/L about 4 h after admission (early) or >3.0 mmol/L at about 12 h after full fluid resuscitation. The card’s ‘after 1 hour of fluid resuscitation’ does not match the original timing; the explanation gives the original timing.
- **APAP-14** — WATCH ‘IV NAC for 12 hours, then repeat the tests’ for repeated ingestion is not universal: the Australian (RCH) guideline, for example, gives the full regimen and rechecks paracetamol and ALT about 2 hours before it ends. Treat as source-specific.

## TOX03A · 8 flags

- **PEST-01** — Card a gives up to 300,000 deaths a year from organophosphates specifically; most sources quote ~250,000–370,000 deaths from pesticide self-poisoning as a whole, with organophosphates the largest share. Card figure kept. The WHO classification quoted is the older oral-LD50 table (the 2019 revision also uses dermal LD50 and reclassifies some compounds) — fine for exam use.
- **PEST-02** — Card lists malathion as dimethyl (correct) and the watch note calls it lipophilic (correct), but malathion is of low mammalian toxicity because of rapid carboxylesterase detoxification; a stem pairing malathion with rapid death is usually testing formulation/solvent or massive dose. No change needed.
- **PEST-09** — Card c gives the atropine challenge as a rise in heart rate >20–25/min with flushing after a small dose. This is traditional teaching from resource-limited settings, not a validated test, and it is absent from Goldfrank's/UpToDate; it should not delay empirical atropine. Card statement kept.
- **PEST-10** — Card c itself notes the maintenance infusion (10–20% of the loading dose per hour) is standard teaching not in Naven's notes — confirmed correct and widely used, so it is explained as standard. Card b's heart rate >80 and systolic >80 are the published atropinisation end-points; some protocols drop the pupil criterion entirely because it is unreliable — the explanation says so.
- **PEST-11** — Card c gives pralidoxime 1–2 g over 30 min with repeat at 1 hour. The WHO regimen is 30 mg/kg (~2 g) over 30 min then 8 mg/kg/h (~500 mg/h) until atropine is no longer needed; both are given, card first. The card implies oximes work if given in time: the Cochrane review (Buckley, Eddleston et al.) concludes the evidence is insufficient to show benefit or harm, and the placebo-controlled trial of the WHO regimen in Sri Lanka found no clinical benefit with a trend to harm, while a small Indian trial of 1 g/h for 48 h found fewer deaths and fewer ventilator days. The explanation now states this rather than asserting benefit.
- **PEST-12** — Card b gives activated charcoal 1 g/kg within 1 hour. Single-dose charcoal in organophosphate poisoning has no proven mortality benefit (negative Sri Lankan RCT) and carries aspiration risk in a patient with vomiting and a falling GCS — reasonable but not mandatory. Card c's glycopyrrolate figures (30% vs 90% confusion, 7.5 mg in 200 mL) come from a small single-centre study; kept as the card states.
- **PEST-12 (FFP)** — Card d calls fresh frozen plasma harmful. The evidence is a single randomised pilot (Pichamuthu et al., Clin Toxicol 2010, Vellore: FFP vs albumin vs saline) in which FFP raised plasma cholinesterase but gave no improvement in deaths (4/19 vs 2/19 saline) or ventilator days (median 10 vs 7.5) — statistically no difference. 'No benefit, do not use' is defensible; 'harmful' overstates it. The explanation says so; if the exam asks for the harmful item on this list, FFP is still the intended answer.
- **PEST-12 (magnesium)** — Card d says magnesium has 'no scientific data'. This is outdated: there are four randomised trials and several cohorts, pooled OR for mortality 0.55 (95% CI 0.32–0.94) in the systematic review by Brvar et al., Clin Toxicol 2018, with hypotension at the highest dose and the conclusion that evidence is insufficient to recommend it. The explanation states 'unproven rather than undocumented'.

## TOX03B · 6 flags

- **PEST-14** — Bromethalin is placed in ‘less toxic’; groupings vary by source and some put it with the moderately or highly toxic agents (it is a potent CNS mitochondrial uncoupler). Thallium and barium carbonate are often listed as highly toxic and are absent. Card kept.
- **PEST-15** — ‘Shock and multi-organ failure within 30 minutes’ is compressed: symptoms (vomiting) start within minutes, but shock usually develops over 1–6 hours and most deaths occur within 24 hours. Yellow-phosphorus staging is also classically given as stage 1 (GI, first 24 h), latent stage (24–72 h), hepatic/renal failure after 72 h. Card kept.
- **PEST-16** — Watch note re ‘vegetable oil lavage’: older forensic texts used liquid paraffin (a non-absorbed mineral oil) to hold phosphorus in the gut, distinct from digestible fats, which increase absorption — the notes may be a garbled version of this. Also, gastric lavage of any kind is not routine in current toxicology guidance. Card kept.
- **PEST-17** — Chlorophacinone is an indandione, not a 4-hydroxycoumarin, so part d’s ‘4-hydroxycoumarin level’ does not strictly cover it. Also, coagulopathy (INR rise) appears at 24–48 h; clinical bleeding may appear later (days). The ‘below 30%’ threshold could not be verified against a standard reference. Card kept.
- **PEST-18** — The 26% PGI figure could not be verified independently; kept as the card states. Part d ‘supportive only’ is correct; note that magnesium trials conflict (some small Indian trials reported benefit, others none), so ‘unproven’ is fair.
- **PEST-19** — Skin contact is ‘not life-threatening’ for intact skin and diluted spray, but fatal systemic poisoning has been reported after prolonged contact with concentrate on damaged skin. Card kept.

## TOX04 · 5 flags

- **REC-01** — Part c ‘In children: inhalants’ — the 2019 national survey’s point is that inhalants are the only class used MORE by children than adults (about 1.17% vs 0.58%); inhalants are not the commonest substance in children overall (alcohol and opioids are reported higher). Worth rewording to ‘used more by children than adults’. Check against the report.
- **REC-03** — Part c ‘maximum of 2 mg’ — 2 mg is not a true ceiling: if no response, doses up to about 10 mg are given before excluding opioid toxicity (more relevant with fentanyl analogues). Consistent with TOX-01, so left as is in the explanation.
- **REC-09** — Part a ‘detected as a benzodiazepine on screening’ — flunitrazepam is often NOT detected on routine urine benzodiazepine immunoassays (low dose, poor cross-reactivity of 7-aminoflunitrazepam); specific assays are needed. Also ‘lasts 4–6 hours’ is short: sedation and amnesia are described as lasting 8–12 hours or more (US DOJ Rohypnol fact sheet: onset 15–20 min, effects >12 h).
- **REC-13** — Part c ‘2 mg per dose to a maximum of 12 mg’ — does not match the standard regimen (Boyer & Shannon, NEJM 2005): 12 mg initially, then 2 mg every 2 hours while symptoms persist, maintenance 8 mg every 6 hours, about 12–32 mg per 24 h. The card already adds the standard regimen; the notes’ line should probably go.
- **REC-15** — WATCH ‘temperature climbs 1 degree every 10 minutes’ — standard texts quote a rise of about 1 °C (up to 2 °C) every 5 minutes in fulminant malignant hyperthermia. ‘All inhaled agents except nitrous oxide’ is better phrased ‘all volatile agents’ (xenon also does not trigger). Onset ‘up to 24 h’ is generous; most cases start intra-operatively or within about an hour after (PACU).

## TOX05 · 10 flags

- **SNAKE-01** — 35,000–50,000 is the older WHO figure. The Indian STG 2017 estimates ~45,900 deaths a year, and the Million Death Study (Suraweera et al., eLife 2020) ~58,000 a year (1.2 million in 2000–2019). The source of ‘34% of proven bites’ could not be found; check it against the notes.
- **SNAKE-02** — ‘Hydrophidae’ is an old family name: sea snakes are now classed within Elapidae (subfamily Hydrophiinae). Their venom is neurotoxic as well as myotoxic, not myotoxic only.
- **SNAKE-03** — Small point: hyaluronidase is the spreading factor; local tissue destruction comes mainly from metalloproteinases, phospholipase A₂ and cytotoxins, which hyaluronidase helps spread.
- **SNAKE-05** — Card matches WHO SEARO for India. Small omissions: syndrome 3 is ‘cobra or king cobra’; syndrome 5 is Russell’s viper for a land bite with coagulopathy (South India, Sri Lanka) and sea snake for a sea bite without; WHO also lists non-Indian kraits (B. niger, B. candidus, B. multicinctus) under syndrome 5.
- **SNAKE-09** — Repeat schedule ‘every 30 min for 3 h, then hourly’ is the older national protocol (2009). The Indian STG 2017 says hourly for 3 h, then 6-hourly up to 24 h, and 6 h after the loading dose of ASV.
- **SNAKE-10** — Doses/route mixed: the Indian STG 2017 gives atropine 0.6 mg then neostigmine 1.5 mg IV, then 0.5 mg with atropine every 30 min for 5 doses (child atropine 0.05 mg/kg, neostigmine 0.04 mg/kg, repeat 0.01 mg/kg); positive = ≥50% recovery of ptosis at 1 h. WHO SEARO uses IM neostigmine (adult ≈0.02 mg/kg, ~1.5 mg). ‘0.5–2 mg’ blends the test dose with the 0.5 mg repeat dose; ‘every half hour for 3 h then hourly’ does not match the STG’s 5 half-hourly doses.
- **SNAKE-11** — Card says SC adrenaline is the only premedication with evidence (correct), but omits that it is now advised routinely: WHO SEARO 2016 recommends it unless the antivenom’s reaction rate is known to be low, and the Indian STG 2017 before ASV except in known hypertensives. STG 2017 gives lyophilised shelf life as 3–5 years.
- **SNAKE-13** — Indian STG 2017 regimens differ from the card: neurotoxic 10 vials over 30 min, second 10 at 1 h if no better (max 20); haemotoxic low-dose 10 vials (Russell’s) or 6 (saw-scaled) then 2 vials 6-hourly, or high-dose 10 then 6 vials 6-hourly. The card’s ‘5–10 vials 6-hourly’ is the older protocol.
- **SNAKE-15** — Qbank fix (WHO SEARO 2016 / Indian STG 2017): IM adrenaline 0.5 mg (child 0.01 mg/kg) is given FIRST at the earliest sign of anaphylaxis, not only ‘if severe’; chlorphenamine and hydrocortisone follow as adjuncts.
- **SNAKE-16** — Myoglobinuria target ‘urine above 100 mL/h’ could not be confirmed in WHO SEARO or the Indian STG 2017 (which describes forced alkaline diuresis cycles expecting ~3 mL/min, within 24 h of the bite).

## TOX06 · 4 flags

- **ONC-06** — Tocilizumab ‘maximum 3 doses’: current guidance (NCCN, ASCO, product labels) is a maximum of 3 doses in 24 hours and 4 doses in total. The WATCH’s ‘older NCI triggers’ (LVEF <40%, creatinine >2.5 × baseline, noradrenaline >2 µg/min for 48 h) are correctly quoted from the NCI criteria (Kroschinsky 2017, Crit Care, Table 3) but are pre-ASTCT and no longer used to decide tocilizumab.
- **ONC-09** — Grade 4 treatment ‘as 3’ implies dexamethasone; standard guidance (ASCO/NCCN) for grade 4 ICANS is high-dose methylprednisolone 1 g/day (usually 3 days) — card omits it.
- **ONC-10** — c: ‘no response in 5–7 days’ — current ASCO/ESMO guidance defines steroid-refractory as no improvement after about 48–72 h of high-dose steroids. e: permanent discontinuation is the rule for grade 4 (and for any-grade myocarditis); after some grade 3 events (e.g. anti-PD-1 colitis) rechallenge can be considered after recovery to grade 1 or less.
- **ONC-11** — ACS row lists trastuzumab: trastuzumab’s signature cardiotoxicity is (usually reversible) LV systolic dysfunction / heart failure, not ACS; the classic ACS-causing cancer drugs are bevacizumab (arterial thrombosis) and fluoropyrimidines (5-FU, capecitabine — vasospasm). GBS/myasthenia row lists sunitinib: supported only by case reports. Myocarditis row omits the key step — permanent discontinuation of the checkpoint inhibitor at any grade, with methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg/day.
